# Synthesis: Dynamic Environment Understanding for Shared Autonomy Power Wheelchairs on Edge Hardware

**Topic context:** Latency, accuracy, use cases, and robustness of pipelines for understanding dynamic environments — in the context of shared autonomy for power wheelchairs deployed on edge hardware (Jetson Orin Nano).

**Shortlist evaluated:** 2026-04-10 | 20 papers shortlisted, 7 excluded.

---

## Major Themes

### Theme 1: The Accuracy-Latency-Edge Tradeoff is Real, Quantifiable, and Architecture-Dependent

The central engineering tension in this literature is not whether dynamic environment understanding is possible on edge hardware, but how much accuracy must be sacrificed to meet real-time latency budgets on constrained processors. Park et al. (2025) provide the most direct evidence: on a Jetson AGX Orin 64GB — the nearest available hardware analogue to the Jetson Orin Nano — the best-performing pipeline (NanoOWL FP16 + EfficientViT-SAM-L0) achieves 47.51 FPS at 84.64% mIoU on RefCOCO+, while slower pipelines using YOLO-World are approximately 43% slower due to PyTorch incompatibility with TensorRT. The paper demonstrates that precision reduction from FP32 to FP16 yields substantial speedups for some architectures but catastrophic mIoU collapse (to near 0) for others — specifically EfficientViT-SAM encoder variants — due to insufficient parameter redundancy. This finding is architectural, not universal: knowledge-distillation-based NanoSAM remains stable across all precision levels while EfficientViT-SAM XL0, XL1, and L2 variants fail catastrophically under FP16 encoder quantization.

Lang et al. (2019) approach the same tradeoff from the LiDAR domain: by encoding point clouds as vertical pillar columns rather than 3D voxels, PointPillars reduces encoding computation sufficiently to enable a lean 2D convolutional backbone that runs at 62 Hz (and 105 Hz in its fastest variant) on standard hardware, outperforming all prior methods on KITTI 3D benchmarks including fusion-based approaches using only LiDAR. The pillar encoding's 2D projection makes it directly compatible with TensorRT optimization on Jetson hardware, a property not shared by volumetric 3D voxel encoders. Similarly, Xu et al. (2025) in LV-DOT explicitly design around the constraint that indoor robots cannot afford sophisticated 3D detection networks, instead fusing multiple lightweight 2D detectors with Kalman-filtered tracking to achieve accuracy comparable to heavier single-model alternatives.

The literature broadly agrees that the tradeoff is architecture-specific rather than monotonic: architectural choices at model design time (encoder structure, representation format, TensorRT compatibility) determine whether edge deployment at real-time rates is feasible at all, before any runtime optimization is applied. What remains unresolved is the precise characterization of this tradeoff on the Jetson Orin Nano specifically — Park et al. (2025) test on the 64GB AGX Orin, which has substantially more GPU and memory resources than the Nano variant. No paper in the shortlist measures any perception pipeline end-to-end on the Orin Nano.

### Theme 2: Sensor Fusion Architectures Split Along a Robustness-Efficiency Axis

The shortlist contains two conceptually distinct fusion strategies, and the papers reveal a fundamental tension between them. The first strategy — tight multi-modal fusion at the feature level — is exemplified by Liu et al. (2023) (BEVFusion, MIT) and Yin et al. (2021) (CenterPoint). BEVFusion achieves a 40x latency reduction in the view transformation bottleneck through optimized BEV pooling, unifying camera and LiDAR in a shared bird's-eye view representation that preserves both geometric precision and semantic density, improving nuScenes 3D detection by 1.3% mAP and BEV segmentation by 13.6% mIoU over prior fusion methods. CenterPoint eliminates the bounding-box-to-track association problem by representing objects as center points and performing greedy closest-point matching, achieving 65.5 NDS and 63.8 AMOTA on nuScenes. Both systems require GPU compute for the fusion transformation and are validated on autonomous driving datasets in outdoor environments.

The second strategy — fault-tolerant loose fusion with independent modality streams — is represented by Liang et al. (2022) (BEVFusion, NeurIPS) and Xu et al. (2025) (LV-DOT). Liang et al. (2022) demonstrate that when camera features are processed independently of LiDAR rather than using LiDAR as a query backbone, the system maintains 15.7–28.9% mAP advantages over alternatives under simulated LiDAR malfunction conditions — the only paper in the shortlist to directly quantify robustness under sensor failure. Xu et al. (2025) take this further for indoor robotics, fusing multiple low-accuracy lightweight detectors rather than one heavy model, using feature-based association and Kalman filtering to aggregate their outputs, and demonstrating that this ensemble fusion outperforms single-model benchmarks across IoU thresholds while remaining feasible on onboard compute. For wheelchair deployment, where sensor occlusion by the user's body, specular reflections from flooring, and unexpected LiDAR dropout are realistic failure modes, the fault-tolerant strategy of Liang et al. (2022) and Xu et al. (2025) is more operationally relevant than the tight fusion approach of Liu et al. (2023) — though tight fusion systems achieve higher peak accuracy when both modalities are functional.

An important unresolved question is whether BEV-space representations — developed for autonomous driving scenarios with wide horizontal fields of view and long detection ranges — are appropriate for wheelchair navigation in narrow indoor corridors at low speeds. The spatial resolution, height range, and voxel granularity assumptions of BEVFusion are calibrated to automotive scenarios and may require non-trivial re-engineering for the wheelchair use case.

### Theme 3: Dynamic Object Segmentation and SLAM Diverge on Learning Dependency vs. Generalization

A recurring methodological fault line runs between learning-based and geometry-based approaches to separating moving objects from the static environment — a prerequisite for accurate localization in dynamic scenes. Chen et al. (2021) (LMNet) establish the learning-based paradigm with range image CNNs that run faster than sensor frame rate on rotating 3D LiDAR, and establish the SemanticKITTI LiDAR-MOS benchmark that subsequent papers use as a shared evaluation standard. Mersch et al. (2022) (4DMOS) advance the state of the art with sparse 4D convolutions applied to voxelized spatiotemporal point clouds, using a receding horizon strategy and binary Bayes filter for online prediction, demonstrating cross-domain generalization from SemanticKITTI to Apollo. Both papers process only geometric information without requiring semantic labels, which improves generalization beyond training-distribution object types.

Bhandari et al. (2024) mount the strongest challenge to this learning-based consensus by demonstrating that a learning-free HMM approach — modeling individual voxels probabilistically and integrating beliefs through HMM filtering — performs competitively with deep learning MOS methods on benchmark datasets while requiring no training data and no retraining for new sensor types or environments. For wheelchair deployment where the target sensor (a specific 2D or 3D LiDAR mounted at wheelchair height) may differ from any training dataset distribution, this cross-sensor generalization without retraining is a significant operational advantage. The HMM approach's weakness is computational: voxel-level probabilistic filtering over an entire map is not obviously faster than CNN inference at sensor frame rate, though latency data are not reported in the paper.

In parallel, the dynamic SLAM literature shows a similar divide. Bescos et al. (2018) (DynaSLAM) and Zhang et al. (2024) (NGD-SLAM) both extend ORB-SLAM2 with dynamic object masking — Bescos et al. using deep learning detection or multi-view geometry for masking with static background inpainting, Zhang et al. using a decoupled mask propagation mechanism to avoid waiting for deep learning inference on every frame. The key distinction is that NGD-SLAM achieves 60 FPS on a laptop CPU alone by separating the mask update rate from the tracking rate, making GPU-free SLAM viable — directly applicable to scenarios where the Jetson Orin Nano's GPU must be reserved for perception inference. Xu et al. (2022) (FAST-LIO2) take a third path: avoiding the dynamic object problem entirely in the SLAM backbone by using direct point cloud registration with an incremental ikd-Tree structure, running at 100 Hz on ARM processors, allowing downstream segmentation to handle dynamic filtering separately. Shan and Englot (2018) (LeGO-LOAM) similarly prioritize computational parsimony through ground-plane segmentation and planar/edge feature extraction with two-step optimization, achieving real-time performance on low-power embedded hardware while matching LOAM accuracy — but without explicit dynamic object handling, which limits robustness in crowded pedestrian environments.

The unresolved question is how deep learning-based dynamic masking (which achieves higher precision in classifying moving objects) trades against geometry-only or HMM-only approaches (which generalize without retraining) under the specific conditions of indoor wheelchair navigation: slow robot velocities, crowded spaces with pedestrians, and low-cost LiDAR sensor types not well represented in KITTI-derived training sets.

### Theme 4: Person Detection and Tracking in Indoor Shared Spaces Has a Distinct Methodological Lineage

While the broader perception literature focuses on 3D object detection for autonomous driving, the wheelchair-relevant literature on detecting and tracking people in indoor shared spaces forms a distinct methodological thread with different sensor modalities, robustness challenges, and evaluation standards. Beyer et al. (2018) originate this thread from the DROW wheelchair dataset — person detection using 2D laser range data, not 3D LiDAR — incorporating a temporal window in the network architecture to exploit sequential leg motion dynamics for disambiguation in crowded environments. The DROW dataset, built from real-world recordings over several days with diverse environments, remains the largest dataset of person annotations in 2D range data. The 2D LiDAR modality used by Beyer et al. (2018) is lower-cost and computationally cheaper than 3D LiDAR, making it specifically relevant to edge-constrained platforms like the Orin Nano.

Lecrosnier et al. (2020) address a complementary sub-problem: detecting not people but navigation-relevant objects — doors and door handles — that a wheelchair user needs to interact with to traverse an indoor environment. Their YOLOv3 + Intel RealSense D435 + SORT pipeline achieves 90% door detection precision and 80% recall at 5.8 FPS on a Jetson TX2, establishing a concrete embedded hardware baseline. The 5.8 FPS performance on TX2 — the predecessor to the Orin series, with substantially less compute — provides a lower bound on what current-generation Jetson hardware should be capable of achieving with the same pipeline. The paper also documents a critical failure mode: door handle recall of 29% due to dataset imbalance, demonstrating that per-class performance gaps can be severe for small or infrequent target classes.

Wojke et al. (2017) (Deep SORT) provide the tracking layer connecting per-frame detections into persistent object identities, reducing identity switches by 45% over SORT baseline through offline-trained deep appearance features used for nearest-neighbor matching. The design choice of offline training is itself an edge deployment strategy: all deep learning compute is front-loaded to offline training, leaving only nearest-neighbor queries in visual feature space at inference time. Lecrosnier et al. (2020) integrate SORT (not Deep SORT) into their wheelchair pipeline, a choice that may reflect the additional inference cost of the appearance feature extractor at 5.8 FPS.

A key tension in this theme is between sensor modality choices: Beyer et al. (2018) demonstrate that 2D LiDAR with temporal context is effective for person detection in crowded spaces, while Lecrosnier et al. (2020) use RGB-D camera for object detection in wheelchair environments. No paper in the shortlist directly compares 2D LiDAR, 3D LiDAR, and RGB-D for person detection specifically in wheelchair use cases — leaving the sensor selection question without an evidence-based answer.

### Theme 5: Trajectory Prediction and Intention Recognition as the Bridge Between Perception and Autonomy

Dynamic environment understanding does not terminate with obstacle detection — for shared autonomy systems, the proximate goal is predicting where dynamic obstacles will move, which determines how the wheelchair's motion planner should respond. Alahi et al. (2016) (Social LSTM) establish the data-driven foundation: using LSTMs with a social pooling layer to jointly model multiple pedestrians' interactions, achieving more than 42% improvement over social force baselines by learning collision avoidance and group movement behaviors from data rather than hand-coding interaction models. This paper establishes the key insight that pedestrian motion in crowded spaces is a multi-agent problem where each agent's trajectory is influenced by others' states — an insight directly applicable to wheelchair navigation in corridors and common areas.

Finean et al. (2022) close the loop from perception through to motion planning in the most complete end-to-end pipeline in the shortlist: RGB-D perception on a Toyota Human Support Robot feeds context-aware trajectory prediction combining intention recognition with trajectory optimization-based motion planning. GPU-accelerated composite distance fields derived from predicted trajectories significantly reduce computation time for the downstream planner, enabling reactive and pre-emptive whole-body motion planning in shared human spaces. The release of the Oxford Indoor Human Motion dataset provides research infrastructure specifically for indoor trajectory prediction — a domain poorly served by outdoor pedestrian datasets. Finean et al. (2022) are also the only paper to explicitly demonstrate that trajectory prediction quality directly affects motion planning outcome quality in a closed-loop validation on a real assistive robot.

The key gap between these papers is latency characterization. Alahi et al. (2016) do not report inference time. Finean et al. (2022) note that GPU acceleration reduces computation time but do not provide specific latency figures for the combined perception-prediction-planning pipeline, or demonstrate that the full system runs at real-time rates on edge hardware. Social LSTM's pooling operation over all agents in a scene scales quadratically with pedestrian count in the worst case — a concern for crowded environments that is not addressed in the original paper and has motivated transformer-based alternatives not in the shortlist. For the wheelchair use case, the key unanswered question is whether intention-aware trajectory prediction can be computed at rates sufficient for safe reactive obstacle avoidance on Jetson Orin Nano class hardware, or whether a simpler constant-velocity Kalman filter model is the only computationally feasible prediction method at the edge.

### Theme 6: Application-Specific Grounding is Sparse — Most Papers Target Autonomous Driving, Not Assistive Robotics

The most consequential structural observation about this literature is that the majority of technically relevant papers are grounded in autonomous driving use cases — outdoor environments, high-speed operation, wide-field sensors, and computational budgets that assume multi-GPU server hardware or high-end automotive compute platforms. Lang et al. (2019), Yin et al. (2021), Liu et al. (2023), and Liang et al. (2022) all use KITTI, nuScenes, or Waymo as their primary benchmarks; Chen et al. (2021) and Mersch et al. (2022) use SemanticKITTI. These datasets feature pedestrians and vehicles at street distances, with LiDAR mounted at vehicle height scanning at 10–20 Hz. Indoor wheelchair navigation operates at fundamentally different scales: shorter detection ranges, lower ceiling heights, slower robot velocities, and a different dominant obstacle class — people at close range from a low-mounted sensor perspective.

Only three papers are directly grounded in wheelchair or indoor assistive robotics deployments: Lecrosnier et al. (2020), validated on an actual wheelchair with Jetson TX2; Xu et al. (2025) (CoNav Chair), a ROS-based shared-control wheelchair platform for indoor social environments; and Beyer et al. (2018), grounded in the wheelchair-derived DROW dataset. Finean et al. (2022) and Xu et al. (2025) (LV-DOT) extend to indoor assistive robot platforms (Toyota HSR and quadcopter respectively) without being wheelchair-specific. The remaining 13 papers must be evaluated for transfer applicability rather than direct relevance.

This application gap has practical consequences. SLAM systems like FAST-LIO2 and LeGO-LOAM are validated on UAV, handheld, and ground vehicle platforms but not specifically on wheelchair-height sensors in narrow indoor corridors where feature density changes significantly. The social pedestrian prediction literature (Alahi et al., 2016) uses overhead camera datasets from pedestrian paths; predictions from a low-mounted wheelchair camera perspective with partial occlusion of leg-level points are a different problem. Lecrosnier et al. (2020) provide the only paper documenting what an embedded wheelchair perception stack actually costs in FPS and precision — and the 5.8 FPS result on Jetson TX2 suggests that the compute headroom on Orin Nano for more sophisticated pipelines requires careful empirical validation, not extrapolation from automotive benchmarks.

## Agreements

**Real-time operation requires architectural alignment with the deployment platform, not just optimization.** Park et al. (2025), Lang et al. (2019), and Zhang et al. (2024) all converge on the finding that achieving real-time inference on edge hardware depends on fundamental architectural choices — TensorRT compatibility, 2D projection of 3D data, CPU/GPU task separation — rather than post-hoc quantization alone. Models not designed with the target platform in mind may fail catastrophically under quantization (Park et al., 2025) or impose memory bottlenecks that no quantization resolves.

**Multi-sensor fusion consistently outperforms single-sensor approaches when both modalities are functional.** Xu et al. (2025), Liu et al. (2023), and Liang et al. (2022) all demonstrate that LiDAR-camera fusion — in varying architectures — achieves higher detection accuracy than either modality alone. Lecrosnier et al. (2020) combine RGB-D depth with visual detection to achieve 3.8% mean depth error on a wheelchair platform. The agreement is on the superiority of fusion; the disagreement is on what fusion architecture is appropriate for constrained hardware (see Contradictions).

**Dynamic object handling is necessary for accurate localization in occupied environments.** Bescos et al. (2018), Zhang et al. (2024), and Bhandari et al. (2024) all demonstrate that SLAM systems operating without dynamic object masking suffer significant localization degradation in environments with moving people. The papers disagree on whether masking should be done via deep learning, geometric consistency, or probabilistic filtering — but agree that unmasked dynamic SLAM is insufficient for reliable navigation in shared spaces.

**Separate detection and tracking are both necessary components.** Every application-oriented perception pipeline in the shortlist uses a detect-then-track paradigm. Wojke et al. (2017) provide the tracking module; Yin et al. (2021) unify detection and tracking in a single model; Lecrosnier et al. (2020) deploy SORT for tracked wheelchair-environment objects; Xu et al. (2025) use Kalman-filtered feature association. No paper advocates detecting moving objects without tracking their trajectories for anticipatory planning.

**Occlusion is a primary robustness challenge in crowded indoor environments.** Beyer et al. (2018) frame occlusion as the defining challenge for person detection in crowded spaces. Wojke et al. (2017) reduce identity switches under occlusion by 45% through appearance features. Bescos et al. (2018) use background inpainting to recover static scene structure occluded by dynamic objects. The shared recognition is that partial observability due to occlusion is not an edge case but the normal operating condition in indoor shared spaces.

**Benchmark datasets from outdoor autonomous driving are insufficient for indoor wheelchair use cases.** Beyer et al. (2018) create a wheelchair-specific dataset (DROW). Chen et al. (2021) create the SemanticKITTI LiDAR-MOS benchmark. Finean et al. (2022) release the Oxford Indoor Human Motion dataset. Each paper recognizes that available public datasets do not adequately represent their target deployment scenario, motivating new dataset creation — a consistent signal that evaluation infrastructure for indoor assistive robotics lags the field.

## Contradictions & Debates

**Deep learning versus learning-free dynamic object segmentation: a false tradeoff?** Chen et al. (2021) and Mersch et al. (2022) establish that deep learning-based moving object segmentation achieves high precision on SemanticKITTI benchmarks. Bhandari et al. (2024) counter that an HMM-based learning-free approach performs competitively on the same benchmarks while generalizing across sensor types without retraining. The contradiction is not over absolute accuracy on benchmark data — where deep learning tends to hold a marginal advantage — but over whether that advantage is preserved when the deployment sensor differs from the training distribution. For the Jetson Orin Nano wheelchair deployment, where the specific LiDAR model (likely a low-cost short-range unit rather than a Velodyne HDL-64) is not represented in SemanticKITTI, the generalization argument of Bhandari et al. (2024) is operationally significant. This debate has not been resolved for indoor short-range LiDAR scenarios.

**Tight multi-modal fusion versus ensemble lightweight fusion for edge constraints.** Liu et al. (2023) demonstrate that tight BEV fusion of camera and LiDAR features achieves state-of-the-art 3D detection, with a 40x latency reduction in the bottleneck view transformation stage. Xu et al. (2025) argue for the opposite architecture: use multiple independent lightweight detectors and fuse their outputs rather than fusing raw sensor features, arguing this avoids the computational demands of sophisticated 3D detection networks while achieving comparable accuracy under IoU thresholds. These papers describe fundamentally different engineering philosophies — pipeline complexity concentrated in feature-space fusion versus distributed across multiple cheap forward passes — and are validated in incompatible scenarios (nuScenes outdoor driving vs. indoor robot navigation). The optimal architecture for an indoor wheelchair at edge compute remains empirically unresolved.

**GPU allocation: unified perception-SLAM versus compute partitioned between SLAM (CPU) and perception (GPU).** Zhang et al. (2024) (NGD-SLAM) explicitly argue for CPU-only SLAM so the GPU can be reserved for other tasks. Bescos et al. (2018) (DynaSLAM) use the GPU for deep learning object masking within the SLAM loop. Finean et al. (2022) use GPU-accelerated distance field computation for motion planning. These papers implicitly assume different compute allocation policies without directly comparing them. For a Jetson Orin Nano system simultaneously running SLAM, dynamic object detection, trajectory prediction, and a shared autonomy decision layer, these resource allocation choices conflict. No paper in the shortlist analyzes the full system compute budget holistically.

**Static ground-plane assumptions in LiDAR SLAM versus wheelchair-height sensor geometry.** Shan and Englot (2018) (LeGO-LOAM) design ground-plane optimization explicitly for ground vehicles, using ground segmentation as a feature filtering step. Xu et al. (2022) (FAST-LIO2) use feature-free direct registration that does not assume ground planarity. In indoor environments with ramps, door thresholds, elevator transitions, and irregular flooring, the LeGO-LOAM assumption of a consistent ground plane may degrade. FAST-LIO2's feature-free approach is more robust to these geometric variations but at potentially higher computational cost. This tradeoff is not characterized for wheelchair-specific indoor environments.

**Trajectory prediction fidelity versus latency on edge hardware.** Alahi et al. (2016) (Social LSTM) demonstrate that modeling pedestrian social interactions substantially improves prediction accuracy over Kalman filter or physics-based baselines. Finean et al. (2022) show that prediction quality directly affects downstream motion planning. However, Social LSTM's pooling over all neighbors scales quadratically with crowd density, and neither paper provides edge-hardware latency data. The implicit assumption in both papers is that GPU compute is available for the prediction module. Whether interaction-aware prediction is feasible at the Jetson Orin Nano's GPU throughput — concurrent with SLAM and detection — is entirely unaddressed. A simpler constant-velocity Kalman prediction may be the only computationally viable option on the Nano, at the cost of accuracy in crowded scenarios.

**2D LiDAR versus 3D LiDAR versus RGB-D for person detection at wheelchair height.** Beyer et al. (2018) demonstrate effective person detection in crowded spaces using 2D laser range data with temporal context. Lecrosnier et al. (2020) use RGB-D (Intel RealSense D435) for door and obstacle detection on a wheelchair. Xu et al. (2025) (LV-DOT) use 3D LiDAR and camera fusion for indoor robot navigation. No paper directly compares these sensor modalities for person detection at wheelchair-eye-level perspective in indoor environments, and the performance differences as a function of crowd density, occlusion level, and sensor mounting height are unknown. This is a directly actionable research gap for the Jetson Orin Nano wheelchair deployment, which must choose a sensor configuration before implementation.

## Open Questions & Research Gaps

**No end-to-end benchmark exists on Jetson Orin Nano for any wheelchair-relevant perception pipeline.** Park et al. (2025) test on the AGX Orin 64GB. Lecrosnier et al. (2020) test on the Jetson TX2. No paper in the shortlist provides latency or accuracy data specifically on the Orin Nano (8GB, lower GPU core count, different memory bandwidth). Extrapolation from the AGX Orin is not safe given Park et al.'s finding that architecture-specific failure modes (FP16 quantization collapse) are not predictable from general performance trends.

**Full system compute budget for concurrent SLAM + detection + prediction + autonomy.** Every paper in the shortlist evaluates a single pipeline component in isolation. No paper models or measures the interaction effects of running SLAM, dynamic object detection, trajectory prediction, and a shared autonomy decision layer concurrently on a single embedded GPU. This integrated system latency is the operationally relevant metric for wheelchair deployment and is entirely absent from the literature.

**Indoor, low-speed, low-LiDAR-height operation remains a domain gap.** SemanticKITTI, nuScenes, and Waymo all assume vehicle-height sensors, outdoor environments, and speeds above 20 km/h. A wheelchair operates at 1–6 km/h indoors at 0.5–1.0m sensor height in environments with different point density, feature distribution, and obstacle proximity profiles. The degree to which models trained on autonomous driving datasets transfer to this scenario — and what fine-tuning or domain adaptation is required — is not quantified in any shortlisted paper.

**Wheelchair-specific person detection dataset is absent.** Beyer et al. (2018) provide person detection data from robot-mounted 2D LiDAR in real environments, but this dataset reflects mobile robot sensor heights rather than wheelchair sensor heights. No paper in the shortlist provides or cites a dataset of person detections from a wheelchair-mounted sensor perspective, with the ground-level geometric distortions and proximity-to-feet effects that entails.

**Sensor failure and degradation modes in indoor environments.** Liang et al. (2022) is the only paper to test under simulated sensor failure, achieving 15.7–28.9% mAP improvements under LiDAR malfunction. But LiDAR failure modes in indoor wheelchair environments — including specular reflection from polished floors, LiDAR interference in multi-robot environments, partial occlusion by the user's body or clothing, and sensor-to-wheelchair vibration — are not studied in any shortlisted paper. For a safety-critical assistive device, formal degradation mode analysis is necessary.

**Occlusion recovery in crowded indoor spaces.** Wojke et al. (2017) show that appearance-based Deep SORT handles longer occlusions than motion-only SORT. Bescos et al. (2018) use inpainting to recover occluded static structure. But neither paper quantifies performance degradation as a function of occlusion duration and crowd density in a wheelchair-specific scenario. For hallways, elevators, and doorways where people pass within centimeters of the chair and occlude most of the field of view, existing tracking evaluations may not predict real behavior.

**Intention recognition for pedestrians in close-proximity wheelchair contexts.** Finean et al. (2022) demonstrate context-aware trajectory prediction combining intention recognition with trajectory optimization in an indoor assistive robot setting, but their validation uses a Toyota HSR in a relatively open lab environment. Pedestrian intention recognition in the immediate vicinity of a power wheelchair — where social interaction distances are compressed, people are often approaching to interact with the wheelchair user, and intent signals (body orientation, gaze direction) are not accessible from a forward-facing low-mounted camera — is not addressed.

**Longitudinal robustness under map changes.** Indoor environments change: furniture is rearranged, new obstacles appear, temporary structures are installed. FAST-LIO2 and LeGO-LOAM are evaluated on sequences where the map is built and used within a single session. No shortlisted paper evaluates long-term SLAM robustness over weeks or months of indoor operation with map drift correction and dynamic map update under structural environment changes — a critical requirement for a wheelchair used daily in a home or care facility.

**Shared autonomy integration of perception outputs.** Xu et al. (2025) (CoNav Chair) provide a reference ROS architecture for shared-control wheelchair navigation with obstacle avoidance, but provide limited quantitative detail on how dynamic object detections (bounding boxes, trajectories) are integrated into the shared autonomy decision layer. The field lacks a well-characterized interface specification between dynamic environment understanding modules and the upstream shared control algorithm — leaving the connection to the shared autonomy literature (reviewed separately in the shared-autonomy-power-wheelchairs synthesis) implicit and unvalidated.

## Methodological Notes

**Dominant evaluation paradigms:**
- **Autonomous driving benchmarks** (SemanticKITTI, nuScenes, KITTI, Waymo): used by 9 of 20 papers. High rigor but poor ecological validity for wheelchair indoor use. Evaluations at vehicle speeds and heights are not transferable without domain adaptation analysis.
- **Controlled indoor lab experiments**: used by Lecrosnier et al. (2020), Finean et al. (2022), Bescos et al. (2018), and Beyer et al. (2018). Highest ecological validity for the wheelchair use case but smaller scale and less reproducible.
- **Abstracted/simulation**: No paper in the shortlist uses simulation exclusively; all validate on real sensors or datasets. This is stronger than the shared autonomy literature, where simulation-only evaluations are common.

**Hardware benchmarks:**
- **Jetson AGX Orin 64GB**: Park et al. (2025) — the only Orin-class hardware benchmark in the set.
- **Jetson TX2**: Lecrosnier et al. (2020) — the only wheelchair-embedded GPU benchmark.
- **Laptop CPU (Intel)**: Zhang et al. (2024) (NGD-SLAM) — relevant as a lower bound on CPU performance.
- **ARM processor compatibility** (no explicit benchmark): Xu et al. (2022) (FAST-LIO2).
- **No paper benchmarks on Jetson Orin Nano specifically.**

**Sensor modality distribution:**
- 3D LiDAR: 10 papers (Xu et al. 2022, Lang et al. 2019, Mersch et al. 2022, Chen et al. 2021, Bhandari et al. 2024, Yin et al. 2021, Liu et al. 2023, Liang et al. 2022, Xu et al. 2025/LV-DOT, Shan/Englot 2018)
- RGB-D camera: 4 papers (Bescos et al. 2018, Finean et al. 2022, Lecrosnier et al. 2020, Zhang et al. 2024)
- 2D LiDAR: 1 paper (Beyer et al. 2018)
- Open-vocabulary camera/VLM: 1 paper (Park et al. 2025)
- Wheelchair-specific sensor configuration: 2 papers (Lecrosnier et al. 2020, Beyer et al. 2018)

**Latency reporting gaps:**
Many papers report FPS or Hz without specifying end-to-end pipeline latency including preprocessing and postprocessing. Papers reporting specific latency numbers: Park et al. (2025) (9.81–45.59ms per stage), Lecrosnier et al. (2020) (5.8 FPS embedded), Lang et al. (2019) (62/105 Hz), Xu et al. (2022) (100 Hz), Zhang et al. (2024) (60 FPS CPU). Papers not reporting latency: Alahi et al. (2016), Finean et al. (2022), Bhandari et al. (2024), Bescos et al. (2018), Xu et al. (2025)/CoNav Chair.

**Dataset geographic/demographic scope:**
All benchmark datasets are from Western Europe (KITTI: Germany; Oxford: UK; nuScenes: Boston/Singapore) or the United States (Waymo). Indoor environments are laboratory or office settings. Healthcare settings (hospitals, care homes), residential environments with assistive device users, and developing-country infrastructure are entirely absent.

**Seminal paper distribution:**
Four papers are flagged as seminal (PointPillars, LeGO-LOAM, DynaSLAM, Social LSTM, Deep SORT). These establish methodological baselines but predate the Jetson Orin hardware generation by 3–7 years; performance figures cited from these papers do not reflect current hardware capabilities.

## Recommended Narrative Arc for Report

The report should move from the edge deployment constraint inward to the application, grounding each technical claim in what is known for the specific Jetson Orin Nano + indoor wheelchair context.

1. **Open with the hardware constraint as the organizing frame** (Park et al., 2025; Lecrosnier et al., 2020): establish that edge deployment is not a default mode for this literature — it is the exception. The Jetson TX2 result (5.8 FPS) and the Orin AGX result (47.51 FPS at 84.64% mIoU) bracket the current hardware generation's capability. Frame the report around the gap between what has been demonstrated and what the Orin Nano specifically can support.

2. **Establish the perception pipeline stack as three separable layers — localization, object detection/segmentation, and trajectory prediction** — and trace how papers address each layer before integrating them. This gives the reader a mental model for understanding which papers plug into which slot:
   - Localization/SLAM: FAST-LIO2, LeGO-LOAM, DynaSLAM, NGD-SLAM
   - Dynamic object detection and segmentation: PointPillars, LV-DOT, LMNet, 4DMOS, HMM-MOS, DeepPerson
   - Prediction and planning integration: Social LSTM, Finean et al., CenterPoint, Deep SORT

3. **Examine the fusion architecture debate** (Liu et al. 2023, Liang et al. 2022, Xu et al. 2025/LV-DOT): frame the tight vs. loose fusion tradeoff as the central design decision for the perception module, and use Liang et al.'s sensor fault tolerance results to argue that for safety-critical assistive devices, the fault-tolerant architecture deserves serious consideration despite lower peak accuracy.
