# Dynamic Environment Understanding Pipelines: Latency, Accuracy, Use Cases, and Robustness for Shared Autonomy in Power Wheelchairs

**Literature Review | 2026-04-10**
**Shortlist evaluated:** 20 papers shortlisted, 7 excluded | Evaluation date: 2026-04-10

---

## Abstract

This literature review examines dynamic environment understanding pipelines for shared autonomy in power wheelchairs, with emphasis on deployment constraints imposed by edge hardware — specifically the NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano. Twenty papers were shortlisted from an evaluated corpus and organized across six major themes: the accuracy-latency-edge tradeoff, sensor fusion architectures, dynamic object segmentation and simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM), person detection and tracking in indoor shared spaces, trajectory prediction and intention recognition, and the application-specific grounding gap between autonomous driving research and assistive robotics. Key findings include: real-time edge performance is architecture-dependent rather than universally achievable through post-hoc quantization (Park et al., 2025); fault-tolerant loose fusion outperforms tight feature-level fusion under sensor degradation conditions relevant to wheelchair deployment (Liang et al., 2022); and learning-free moving object segmentation offers competitive accuracy with stronger cross-sensor generalization (Bhandari et al., 2024). Critical gaps persist: no published benchmark measures any wheelchair-relevant perception pipeline end-to-end on the Jetson Orin Nano, no paper analyzes the full concurrent system compute budget, and wheelchair-height indoor sensor datasets remain absent from the literature.

## Themes

### Theme 1: The Accuracy-Latency-Edge Tradeoff is Real, Quantifiable, and Architecture-Dependent

The central engineering tension in this literature is not whether dynamic environment understanding is possible on edge hardware, but how much accuracy must be sacrificed to meet real-time latency budgets on constrained processors — and whether that sacrifice is even architecturally possible for a given model family. Park et al. (2025) provide the most direct evidence on Orin-class hardware: the best-performing pipeline combining NanoOWL FP16 detection with EfficientViT-SAM-L0 segmentation achieves 47.51 FPS at 84.64% mIoU on the RefCOCO+ benchmark, while pipelines built on YOLO-World run approximately 43% slower due to PyTorch incompatibility with TensorRT. Critically, Park et al. (2025) demonstrate that precision reduction from FP32 to FP16 yields substantial speedups for some architectures but catastrophic mIoU collapse to near zero for others — specifically EfficientViT-SAM encoder variants — due to insufficient parameter redundancy at reduced precision; this failure is architectural and not predictable from general performance trends alone.

Lang et al. (2019) approach the same tradeoff from the LiDAR domain with a design that encodes point clouds as vertical pillar columns rather than 3D voxels, reducing encoding computation sufficiently to enable a lean 2D convolutional backbone that runs at 62 Hz — and up to 105 Hz in its fastest variant — while outperforming all prior methods on the KITTI 3D and bird's-eye-view benchmarks, including fusion-based approaches that use only LiDAR. The pillar encoding's 2D projection makes it directly compatible with TensorRT optimization on Jetson hardware, a property not shared by volumetric 3D voxel encoders. Xu et al. (2025a) in LV-DOT explicitly design around the constraint that indoor robots cannot afford sophisticated 3D detection networks, instead fusing multiple lightweight 2D detectors with Kalman-filtered tracking to achieve accuracy comparable to heavier single-model alternatives at indoor-robot-feasible compute costs. The literature broadly agrees that the tradeoff is architecture-specific rather than monotonic: architectural choices at model design time — encoder structure, representation format, TensorRT compatibility — determine whether edge deployment at real-time rates is feasible at all, before any runtime optimization is applied. What remains unresolved is the precise characterization of this tradeoff on the Jetson Orin Nano specifically, since Park et al. (2025) test on the Jetson AGX Orin 64GB, which has substantially more GPU and memory resources than the Nano variant, and no paper in the shortlist measures any perception pipeline end-to-end on the Orin Nano.

### Theme 2: Sensor Fusion Architectures Split Along a Robustness-Efficiency Axis

The shortlist contains two conceptually distinct fusion strategies, and the papers reveal a fundamental tension between them. The first strategy — tight multi-modal fusion at the feature level — is exemplified by Liu et al. (2023) and Yin et al. (2021). Liu et al. (2023) achieve a 40x reduction in view transformation latency through optimized BEV pooling, unifying camera and LiDAR in a shared bird's-eye view representation that preserves both geometric precision and semantic density, improving nuScenes 3D detection by 1.3% mAP and BEV map segmentation by 13.6% mIoU over prior fusion methods. Yin et al. (2021) eliminate the bounding-box-to-track association problem by representing objects as center points and performing greedy closest-point matching, achieving 65.5 NDS and 63.8 AMOTA on nuScenes — both systems requiring GPU compute for fusion transformations and validated primarily in autonomous driving scenarios.

The second strategy — fault-tolerant loose fusion with independent modality streams — is represented by Liang et al. (2022) and Xu et al. (2025a). Liang et al. (2022) demonstrate that when camera features are processed independently of LiDAR rather than using LiDAR as a query backbone, the system maintains 15.7–28.9% mAP advantages over alternatives under simulated LiDAR malfunction conditions, making it the only paper in the shortlist to directly quantify robustness under sensor failure. Xu et al. (2025a) take this further for indoor robotics by fusing multiple low-accuracy lightweight detectors rather than one heavy model, using feature-based association and Kalman filtering to aggregate outputs, and demonstrating that this ensemble fusion outperforms single-model benchmarks across IoU thresholds while remaining feasible on onboard compute. For wheelchair deployment, where sensor occlusion by the user's body, specular reflections from flooring, and unexpected LiDAR dropout are realistic failure modes, the fault-tolerant strategy is more operationally relevant than tight fusion, even though tight fusion systems achieve higher peak accuracy when both modalities are functional (Liu et al., 2023). An important unresolved question is whether BEV-space representations — developed for autonomous driving with wide horizontal fields of view and long detection ranges — are appropriate for wheelchair navigation in narrow indoor corridors at low speeds, where the spatial resolution, height range, and voxel granularity assumptions may require non-trivial re-engineering.

### Theme 3: Dynamic Object Segmentation and SLAM Diverge on Learning Dependency Versus Generalization

A recurring methodological fault line separates learning-based from geometry-based or probabilistic approaches to separating moving objects from the static environment — a prerequisite for accurate localization in dynamic scenes. Chen et al. (2021) establish the learning-based paradigm with range image CNNs (LMNet) that run faster than sensor frame rate on rotating 3D LiDAR and establish the SemanticKITTI LiDAR-MOS benchmark that subsequent papers adopt as the shared evaluation standard. Mersch et al. (2022) advance the state of the art with sparse 4D convolutions applied to voxelized spatiotemporal point clouds, using a receding horizon strategy and binary Bayes filter for online prediction, demonstrating cross-domain generalization from SemanticKITTI to Apollo — both papers processing only geometric information without semantic labels, which improves generalization beyond training-distribution object types. Bhandari et al. (2024) mount the strongest challenge to this learning-based consensus by demonstrating that a learning-free HMM approach — modeling individual voxels probabilistically and integrating beliefs through HMM filtering — performs competitively with deep learning MOS methods on the same benchmarks while requiring no training data and no retraining when the deployment sensor differs from any training distribution.

The dynamic SLAM literature reveals a parallel divide. Bescos et al. (2018) extend ORB-SLAM2 with dynamic object masking via deep learning detection or multi-view geometry and static background inpainting, demonstrating that this hybrid approach substantially outperforms unmodified visual SLAM in highly dynamic environments. Zhang et al. (2024) address the same problem differently, decoupling the mask propagation mechanism from the tracking loop so that deep learning inference does not block frame-rate tracking — achieving 60 FPS on a laptop CPU alone and making GPU-free dynamic SLAM viable for scenarios where the Jetson Orin Nano's GPU must be reserved for perception inference. Xu et al. (2022) take a third path entirely, avoiding the dynamic object problem in the SLAM backbone by using direct point cloud registration with an incremental ikd-Tree structure running at up to 100 Hz on ARM processors, with ARM processor compatibility explicitly documented, allowing downstream segmentation to handle dynamic filtering separately. Shan and Englot (2018) similarly prioritize computational parsimony through ground-plane segmentation and two-step Levenberg-Marquardt optimization to achieve real-time embedded-hardware performance matching LOAM accuracy, but without explicit dynamic object handling — limiting robustness in crowded pedestrian environments. The unresolved question is how deep learning-based dynamic masking trades against geometry-only or HMM-only approaches under the specific conditions of indoor wheelchair navigation: slow robot velocities, crowded spaces, and low-cost LiDAR sensor types not represented in KITTI-derived training sets.

### Theme 4: Person Detection and Tracking in Indoor Shared Spaces Has a Distinct Methodological Lineage

While the broader perception literature focuses on 3D object detection for autonomous driving, the wheelchair-relevant literature on detecting and tracking people in indoor shared spaces forms a distinct methodological thread with different sensor modalities, robustness challenges, and evaluation standards. Beyer et al. (2018) originate this thread directly from the DROW wheelchair dataset — person detection using 2D laser range data, not 3D LiDAR — incorporating a temporal window in the network architecture to exploit sequential leg motion dynamics for disambiguation in crowded environments, creating the largest dataset of person annotations in 2D range data from real-world environments and demonstrating superior performance over three baseline methods across occlusion, dynamic leg motion, and crowding conditions. The 2D LiDAR modality used by Beyer et al. (2018) is lower-cost and computationally cheaper than 3D LiDAR, making it specifically relevant to edge-constrained platforms like the Orin Nano.

Lecrosnier et al. (2020) address a complementary sub-problem: detecting not people but navigation-relevant objects — doors and door handles — that a wheelchair user must interact with to traverse an indoor environment. Their YOLOv3 + Intel RealSense D435 + SORT pipeline achieves 90% door detection precision and 3.8% mean depth estimation error at 5.8 FPS on a Jetson TX2, establishing the only concrete embedded hardware baseline in the shortlist for a wheelchair perception stack; the 5.8 FPS on the TX2 predecessor to the Orin series provides an empirical lower bound that current-generation Jetson hardware should exceed, but the paper also documents that door handle recall falls to 29% due to dataset imbalance — demonstrating that per-class performance gaps can be severe for small or infrequent target classes. Wojke et al. (2017) provide the tracking layer that connects per-frame detections into persistent object identities, reducing identity switches by 45% over the SORT baseline through offline-trained deep appearance features used for nearest-neighbor matching at inference time; the design choice of offline training is itself an edge deployment strategy, front-loading all deep learning compute to the training phase and leaving only fast nearest-neighbor queries at inference. Lecrosnier et al. (2020) integrate SORT rather than Deep SORT into their wheelchair pipeline, a choice that likely reflects the additional inference cost of the appearance feature extractor at 5.8 FPS. A key unresolved tension in this theme is between sensor modality choices: no paper in the shortlist directly compares 2D LiDAR, 3D LiDAR, and RGB-D for person detection at wheelchair-eye-level perspective in indoor environments with varying crowd density and occlusion levels, leaving the sensor selection question without an evidence-based answer.

### Theme 5: Trajectory Prediction and Intention Recognition as the Bridge Between Perception and Autonomy

Dynamic environment understanding does not terminate with obstacle detection — for shared autonomy systems, the proximate goal is predicting where dynamic obstacles will move, which determines how the wheelchair's motion planner should respond. Alahi et al. (2016) establish the data-driven foundation with Social LSTM: using LSTMs with a social pooling layer to jointly model multiple pedestrians' interactions, the method achieves more than 42% improvement over social force model baselines by learning collision avoidance and group movement behaviors from data, establishing the key insight that pedestrian motion in crowded spaces is a multi-agent problem where each agent's trajectory is influenced by others' states — directly applicable to wheelchair navigation in corridors and common areas. The social pooling operation, however, scales quadratically with pedestrian count in the worst case, a concern for crowded environments not addressed in the original paper.

Finean et al. (2022) close the loop from perception through to motion planning in the most complete end-to-end pipeline in the shortlist: RGB-D perception on a Toyota Human Support Robot feeds context-aware trajectory prediction combining intention recognition with trajectory optimization-based motion planning, while GPU-accelerated composite distance fields derived from predicted trajectories reduce computation time for the downstream planner, enabling reactive and pre-emptive whole-body motion planning in shared human spaces. Finean et al. (2022) are also the only paper to explicitly demonstrate that trajectory prediction quality directly affects motion planning outcome quality in a closed-loop validation on a real assistive robot, and their release of the Oxford Indoor Human Motion dataset provides research infrastructure specifically for indoor trajectory prediction — a domain poorly served by outdoor pedestrian datasets. The key gap between these papers and the wheelchair deployment target is latency characterization: Alahi et al. (2016) do not report inference time, and Finean et al. (2022) note that GPU acceleration reduces computation time without providing specific latency figures for the combined perception-prediction-planning pipeline or demonstrating that the full system runs at real-time rates on edge hardware. Whether intention-aware trajectory prediction can be computed at rates sufficient for safe reactive obstacle avoidance on Jetson Orin Nano hardware — concurrent with SLAM and detection — or whether a simpler constant-velocity Kalman filter is the only computationally feasible prediction method at the edge remains entirely unaddressed.

### Theme 6: Application-Specific Grounding is Sparse — Most Papers Target Autonomous Driving, Not Assistive Robotics

The most consequential structural observation about this literature is that the majority of technically relevant papers are grounded in autonomous driving use cases — outdoor environments, high-speed operation, wide-field sensors, and computational budgets that assume multi-GPU server hardware or high-end automotive compute platforms. Lang et al. (2019), Yin et al. (2021), Liu et al. (2023), and Liang et al. (2022) all use KITTI, nuScenes, or Waymo as their primary benchmarks; Chen et al. (2021) and Mersch et al. (2022) use SemanticKITTI. These datasets feature pedestrians and vehicles at street distances, with LiDAR mounted at vehicle height scanning at 10–20 Hz — whereas indoor wheelchair navigation operates at 1–6 km/h at 0.5–1.0m sensor height in environments with different point density, feature distribution, and obstacle proximity profiles.

Only three papers are directly grounded in wheelchair or indoor assistive robotics deployments: Lecrosnier et al. (2020), validated on an actual wheelchair platform with Jetson TX2; Xu et al. (2025b) (CoNav Chair), a ROS-based shared-control wheelchair platform for indoor social environments emphasizing user trust and autonomy; and Beyer et al. (2018), grounded in the wheelchair-derived DROW dataset. Finean et al. (2022) and Xu et al. (2025a) extend to indoor assistive robot platforms — a Toyota HSR and a physical quadcopter, respectively — without being wheelchair-specific. The remaining 13 papers must be evaluated for transfer applicability rather than direct relevance. This application gap has practical consequences: SLAM systems like FAST-LIO2 (Xu et al., 2022) and LeGO-LOAM (Shan & Englot, 2018) are validated on UAV, handheld, and ground vehicle platforms but not specifically on wheelchair-height sensors in narrow indoor corridors where feature density and ground-plane geometry change significantly. The social pedestrian prediction literature (Alahi et al., 2016) uses overhead camera datasets from open pedestrian paths; predictions from a low-mounted wheelchair camera perspective with partial leg-level occlusion constitute a fundamentally different geometric problem. Lecrosnier et al. (2020) provide the only paper documenting what an embedded wheelchair perception stack actually costs in FPS and precision, and the 5.8 FPS result on the TX2 suggests that the compute headroom on Orin Nano for more sophisticated pipelines requires careful empirical validation rather than optimistic extrapolation from automotive benchmarks.

## Key Papers

**Park, J., Kim, P., & Ko, D. (2025)** — [Real-time open-vocabulary perception for mobile robots on edge devices: a systematic analysis of the accuracy-latency trade-off](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12583037/)
*Frontiers in Robotics and AI, 2025*
This paper provides a systematic benchmark of open-vocabulary visual perception pipelines on the NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin 64GB, evaluating combinations of NanoOWL and YOLO-World detection models paired with NanoSAM and EfficientViT-SAM segmentation models at FP32, FP16, and INT8 TensorRT precision. The best-performing pipeline — NanoOWL FP16 with EfficientViT-SAM-L0 — achieves 47.51 FPS at 84.64% mIoU on RefCOCO+, while certain EfficientViT-SAM encoder variants suffer catastrophic mIoU collapse under FP16 quantization due to insufficient parameter redundancy. This paper is uniquely actionable for Jetson Orin Nano deployment decisions as the only paper in the shortlist to benchmark open-vocabulary perception end-to-end on Orin-class hardware with explicit latency figures and TensorRT precision trade-off analysis.

**Xu, W., Cai, Y., He, D., Lin, J., & Zhang, F. (2022)** — [FAST-LIO2: Fast Direct LiDAR-inertial Odometry](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.06829)
*IEEE Transactions on Robotics, 2022*
FAST-LIO2 registers raw point clouds directly to maps without feature extraction and employs an incremental k-d tree (ikd-Tree) for dynamic map maintenance, operating at up to 100 Hz with documented compatibility on ARM-based processors including those found in Jetson-class hardware. Across 19 sequences from multiple LiDAR datasets, FAST-LIO2 achieves consistently higher accuracy at lower computational load than competing systems, including those using feature extraction as an intermediate step. Its feature-free, ARM-compatible design makes it a strong candidate SLAM backbone for wheelchair deployment on the Jetson Orin Nano, where compute must be shared with perception and prediction modules.

**Zhang, Y., Bujanca, M., & Lujan, M. (2024)** — [NGD-SLAM: Towards Real-Time Dynamic SLAM without GPU](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.07392)
*arXiv preprint (cs.RO) / IROS 2025*
NGD-SLAM decouples camera tracking from deep learning-based dynamic masking via a mask propagation mechanism, combining ORB feature tracking with optical flow in a hybrid strategy that achieves 60 FPS SLAM on a laptop CPU alone while maintaining high localization accuracy in dynamic environments. The GPU-free design is directly applicable to scenarios where the Jetson Orin Nano's GPU must be reserved for perception inference such as object detection or trajectory prediction. This is the first SLAM system to demonstrate feasibility of deep learning for dynamic SLAM without GPU support, establishing a CPU-SLAM baseline for resource-partitioned edge systems.

**Lang, A. H., Vora, S., Caesar, H., Zhou, L., Yang, J., & Beijbom, O. (2019)** — [PointPillars: Fast Encoders for Object Detection from Point Clouds](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.05784)
*CVPR 2019*
PointPillars encodes 3D point clouds as vertical pillar columns via PointNets, enabling a lean 2D convolutional detection backbone that runs at 62 Hz — and 105 Hz in the fastest variant — while outperforming all prior methods on KITTI 3D and bird's-eye-view benchmarks including fusion-based approaches that use only LiDAR. The 2D pillar projection is directly compatible with TensorRT optimization on Jetson hardware in a way that volumetric 3D voxel encoders are not, making PointPillars the canonical lightweight LiDAR detection architecture for edge deployment. Its widespread adoption as a backbone in downstream robotics systems — including wheelchair and mobile robot platforms — makes it an essential baseline reference for any LiDAR-based dynamic obstacle detection pipeline.

**Xu, Z., Shen, H., Han, X., Jin, H., Ye, K., & Shimada, K. (2025)** — [LV-DOT: LiDAR-visual dynamic obstacle detection and tracking for autonomous robot navigation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.20607)
*arXiv preprint (cs.RO), 2025*
LV-DOT fuses outputs from multiple lightweight, individually low-accuracy detectors — rather than a single sophisticated 3D detection network — using feature-based association and Kalman filtering with a dynamic obstacle classification algorithm, validated on datasets and physical quadcopter experiments. This ensemble fusion architecture outperforms single-model benchmarks across IoU thresholds while remaining feasible on onboard compute, directly targeting the indoor robot navigation scenario under compute constraints. The LiDAR-camera fusion design with Kalman-filtered tracking mirrors the sensor configuration typical of smart wheelchairs, making LV-DOT the most operationally analogous recent paper to the target deployment scenario.

Superior performance over three baseline methods is demonstrated explicitly under occlusion, dynamic leg motion, and crowding conditions — the dominant challenge conditions for power wheelchair navigation. The DROW dataset origin in wheelchair recording contexts and the use of computationally cheaper 2D LiDAR make this paper uniquely relevant to indoor edge-constrained wheelchair perception systems.

*IROS 2018*
LeGO-LOAM uses point cloud segmentation to filter noise followed by planar and edge feature extraction, applying a two-step Levenberg-Marquardt optimization to achieve real-time six-DOF pose estimation on low-power embedded systems with accuracy matching or exceeding the LOAM baseline. ## Contradictions & Debates

### Deep Learning Versus Learning-Free Dynamic Object Segmentation

Chen et al. (2021) and Mersch et al. (2022) establish that deep learning-based moving object segmentation achieves high precision on SemanticKITTI benchmarks using learned spatiotemporal representations — range image CNNs in the former case and sparse 4D convolutions in the latter. Bhandari et al. (2024) counter that a learning-free HMM approach performs competitively on the same benchmarks while requiring no training data and no retraining when the deployment sensor differs from any training distribution. The contradiction is not over absolute accuracy on benchmark data, where deep learning tends to hold a marginal advantage, but over whether that advantage is preserved when the deployment sensor and environment differ from the training distribution. For the Jetson Orin Nano wheelchair deployment, where the target LiDAR (likely a low-cost short-range unit mounted below standard vehicle height) is not represented in SemanticKITTI, the generalization argument of Bhandari et al. (2024) carries operational weight that benchmark-only comparisons do not capture. This debate has not been resolved for indoor short-range LiDAR scenarios, and the HMM method's computational profile under conditions of dense voxel maps in large indoor environments has not been characterized.

### Tight Multi-Modal Fusion Versus Ensemble Lightweight Fusion for Edge Constraints

Liu et al. (2023) demonstrate that tight BEV fusion of camera and LiDAR features — with a 40x latency reduction in the bottleneck view transformation stage — achieves state-of-the-art 3D detection performance at high accuracy when both modalities are functional. Xu et al. (2025a) argue for the opposite architecture: use multiple independent lightweight detectors and fuse their detection outputs rather than their raw features, avoiding the computational demands of sophisticated 3D detection networks while achieving comparable accuracy across IoU thresholds. These papers describe fundamentally different engineering philosophies — pipeline complexity concentrated in feature-space fusion versus distributed across multiple cheap forward passes — and are validated in incompatible scenarios: nuScenes outdoor driving versus indoor robot navigation on onboard compute. Liang et al. (2022) add a third dimension to this debate by demonstrating that tight fusion architectures that treat LiDAR as a backbone query are fragile under sensor failure, while independent processing of each modality maintains substantial accuracy margins under malfunction. The optimal fusion architecture for an indoor wheelchair at edge compute remains empirically unresolved, as no paper tests any fusion architecture on indoor wheelchair-scale scenarios with simultaneous edge compute and sensor degradation constraints.

### GPU Allocation: Unified Perception-SLAM Versus Compute Partitioned Between SLAM and Perception

Zhang et al. (2024) explicitly argue for CPU-only SLAM so the GPU can be reserved for perception inference such as object detection and trajectory prediction. Bescos et al. (2018) use the GPU for deep learning-based dynamic object masking within the SLAM loop itself, tightly coupling SLAM and perception on the same GPU compute. Finean et al. (2022) further allocate GPU resources to trajectory prediction and motion planning distance field computation downstream of SLAM. These papers implicitly assume different compute allocation policies without directly comparing them, and none analyzes the full system compute budget under concurrent operation. For a Jetson Orin Nano system simultaneously running SLAM, dynamic object detection, trajectory prediction, and a shared autonomy decision layer, these resource allocation choices conflict: GPU cycles spent inside the SLAM loop (Bescos et al., 2018) are not available for perception inference (Xu et al., 2025a), and no paper in the shortlist has characterized the interaction effects of running all pipeline components concurrently on a single embedded GPU.

### Static Ground-Plane Assumptions in LiDAR SLAM Versus Wheelchair-Height Sensor Geometry

Shan and Englot (2018) design ground-plane optimization explicitly for ground vehicles, using ground segmentation as a feature filtering step to reduce noise and improve pose estimation efficiency. Xu et al. (2022) use feature-free direct registration that does not assume ground planarity, making FAST-LIO2 more robust to geometric variations but potentially at higher computational cost. In indoor wheelchair environments with ramps, door thresholds, elevator transitions, and irregular flooring — all common in accessible built environments — the LeGO-LOAM assumption of a consistent ground plane may degrade localization accuracy in exactly the situations most relevant to wheelchair navigation. This tradeoff is not characterized for wheelchair-specific indoor environments by either paper, and neither provides performance data from sensor heights representative of wheelchair mounting configurations.

### Trajectory Prediction Fidelity Versus Latency on Edge Hardware

Alahi et al. (2016) demonstrate that modeling pedestrian social interactions substantially improves prediction accuracy over Kalman filter or physics-based baselines, with the social pooling mechanism learning collision avoidance and group behaviors from real pedestrian data. Finean et al. (2022) provide evidence that prediction quality directly affects downstream motion planning outcome quality in a real indoor assistive robot, strengthening the case for high-fidelity prediction in shared autonomy systems. However, Social LSTM's pooling scales quadratically with pedestrian count, and neither paper provides edge-hardware latency data or demonstrates that interaction-aware prediction runs at real-time rates concurrent with SLAM and detection. The implicit assumption in both papers is that GPU compute is available for the prediction module without competition from other pipeline components. Whether interaction-aware prediction is feasible at the Jetson Orin Nano's GPU throughput under concurrent system load is entirely unaddressed, leaving open the question of whether a simpler constant-velocity Kalman prediction is the only computationally viable option at the edge.

### 2D LiDAR Versus 3D LiDAR Versus RGB-D for Person Detection at Wheelchair Height

Beyer et al. (2018) demonstrate effective person detection in crowded spaces using 2D laser range data with temporal context derived from the wheelchair-specific DROW dataset, without requiring the volumetric information of 3D LiDAR. Lecrosnier et al. (2020) use RGB-D (Intel RealSense D435) for door and obstacle detection on a wheelchair, achieving 90% door detection precision at 5.8 FPS on embedded hardware. Xu et al. (2025a) use 3D LiDAR and camera fusion for indoor robot navigation at higher computational cost. No paper in the shortlist directly compares these sensor modalities for person detection at wheelchair-eye-level perspective in indoor environments, and the performance differences as a function of crowd density, occlusion level, and sensor mounting height are unknown. This is a directly actionable research gap for the Jetson Orin Nano wheelchair deployment, which must choose a sensor configuration before implementation — and the evidence base does not currently support a principled choice between 2D LiDAR, 3D LiDAR, and RGB-D for the specific geometric and computational constraints of wheelchair-mounted sensing.

## Open Questions & Research Gaps

- **No end-to-end benchmark exists on Jetson Orin Nano for any wheelchair-relevant perception pipeline.** Park et al. (2025) test on the Jetson AGX Orin 64GB, and Lecrosnier et al. (2020) test on the older Jetson TX2; no paper benchmarks on the Orin Nano (8GB, lower GPU core count, different memory bandwidth). Extrapolation from AGX Orin results is unsafe given Park et al.'s finding that architecture-specific quantization failure modes are not predictable from general performance trends, making Orin Nano characterization the highest-priority near-term empirical task.

- **Full system compute budget for concurrent SLAM, detection, prediction, and autonomy.** Every paper in the shortlist evaluates a single pipeline component in isolation. No paper models or measures the interaction effects of running SLAM (Xu et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2024), dynamic object detection (Xu et al., 2025a; Lang et al., 2019), trajectory prediction (Alahi et al., 2016; Finean et al., 2022), and a shared autonomy decision layer (Xu et al., 2025b) concurrently on a single embedded GPU. This integrated system latency is the operationally relevant metric for wheelchair deployment and is entirely absent from the literature.

- **Indoor, low-speed, low-LiDAR-height operation remains a domain gap.** SemanticKITTI, nuScenes, and Waymo all assume vehicle-height sensors, outdoor environments, and speeds above 20 km/h. A wheelchair operates at 1–6 km/h indoors at 0.5–1.0m sensor height with different point density, feature distribution, and obstacle proximity profiles. The degree to which models trained on these datasets transfer to the wheelchair scenario — and what fine-tuning or domain adaptation is required — is not quantified in any shortlisted paper; Chen et al. (2021) and Mersch et al. (2022) gesture toward the problem without addressing it.

- **Wheelchair-specific person detection dataset is absent.** Beyer et al. (2018) provide person detection data from robot-mounted 2D LiDAR in real environments, but the sensor heights and viewpoints reflect mobile robot configurations rather than wheelchair configurations. No paper provides or cites a dataset of person detections from a wheelchair-mounted sensor perspective with the ground-level geometric distortions and proximity-to-feet effects that wheelchair-height mounting entails, creating a dataset gap that limits supervised learning approaches for the target scenario.

- **Sensor failure and degradation modes in indoor environments are unstudied.** Liang et al. (2022) is the only paper to test under simulated sensor failure, achieving 15.7–28.9% mAP improvements under LiDAR malfunction. However, LiDAR failure modes specific to indoor wheelchair environments — specular reflection from polished floors, LiDAR interference in multi-robot hospital settings, partial occlusion by the user's body or clothing, and sensor-to-wheelchair vibration — are not studied in any shortlisted paper. For a safety-critical assistive device where perception failure directly endangers the user, formal degradation mode analysis is a prerequisite for clinical deployment.

- **Occlusion recovery performance under dense crowd conditions.** Wojke et al. (2017) show that appearance-based Deep SORT handles longer occlusions than motion-only SORT, and Bescos et al. (2018) use inpainting to recover occluded static structure. Neither paper quantifies performance degradation as a function of occlusion duration and crowd density in a wheelchair-specific scenario — hallways, elevators, and doorways where people pass within centimeters of the chair and may occlude most of the sensor's field of view. Existing tracking evaluations are conducted at distances and crowd densities that may not predict behavior in close-proximity wheelchair interactions.

- **Intention recognition for pedestrians in close-proximity wheelchair contexts.** Finean et al. (2022) demonstrate context-aware trajectory prediction combining intention recognition with trajectory optimization in an indoor assistive robot setting, but their validation uses a Toyota HSR in a relatively open laboratory environment. Pedestrian intention recognition in the immediate vicinity of a power wheelchair — where social interaction distances are compressed, people frequently approach to interact with the wheelchair user, and intent signals such as body orientation and gaze direction are not accessible from a forward-facing low-mounted camera — is not addressed by any paper in the shortlist.

- **Longitudinal robustness under map changes.** FAST-LIO2 (Xu et al., 2022) and LeGO-LOAM (Shan & Englot, 2018) are evaluated on sequences where the map is built and used within a single session. No shortlisted paper evaluates long-term SLAM robustness over weeks or months of indoor operation with map drift correction and dynamic map updates under structural environment changes — furniture rearrangement, temporary construction, new obstacles — all of which are routine in home, office, and care facility environments where wheelchairs operate daily.

- **Shared autonomy integration interface for perception outputs.** Xu et al. (2025b) (CoNav Chair) provide a reference ROS architecture for shared-control wheelchair navigation with obstacle avoidance but provide limited quantitative detail on how dynamic object detections, bounding boxes, and trajectories are integrated into the shared autonomy decision layer. The field lacks a well-characterized interface specification between dynamic environment understanding modules and the upstream shared control algorithm, leaving the connection to the shared autonomy literature implicit and unvalidated in any end-to-end system.
