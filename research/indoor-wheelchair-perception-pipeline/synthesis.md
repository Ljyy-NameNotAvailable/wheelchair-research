# Synthesis: Real-Time Non-LLM Indoor Perception Pipeline for Shared Autonomy Power Wheelchair

**Topic:** YOLO-based detection, multi-object tracking, RGB-D + 2D LiDAR sensor fusion, and edge deployment on Jetson Orin Nano
**Shortlist papers:** 18 shortlisted (14 excluded with rationale)
**Synthesis date:** 2026-04-10

---

## Major Themes

### Theme 1: The Jetson Orin Nano Is a Viable but Constrained Execution Target — if TensorRT Optimization Is Applied

The central engineering question for the pipeline is whether real-time perception is achievable within the Jetson Orin Nano's ~20 TOPS INT8 budget. The shortlisted benchmarks converge on a qualified yes, but with important caveats about optimization discipline. Alqahtani et al. (2024) provide the most direct validation: YOLOv8n achieves 23 ms latency with INT8 quantization on Jetson Orin Nano, which fits within the pipeline's 50 ms safety loop budget even before tracker and LiDAR processing are added. Rey et al. (2025) report 52–65 FPS for YOLOv8n on Jetson Orin NX (one tier above the Nano) with TensorRT INT8, setting the performance ceiling while confirming that sub-Nano hardware (Raspberry Pi 5) fails to meet real-time requirements entirely.

However, these benchmarks were collected for single-model workloads. The wheelchair pipeline must run YOLO, a tracker, DR-SPAAM LiDAR detection, and SLAM concurrently. Panopticus (Lee et al., 2024) directly confronts this multi-task reality, designing an adaptive multi-branch detection scheme that maintains a 33 ms latency target on Jetson Orin Nano (explicitly tested on the 6.3 GB model) by dynamically shedding model branches under resource pressure. Pham et al. (2023) add a cross-generational comparison: the Orin Nano delivers 15% better throughput than its predecessor (Jetson Nano) at 50% lower energy consumption, while achieving 47.56 FPS on a full video-analysis pipeline with only 3.11 GB RAM. This headroom leaves meaningful compute budget for the wheelchair's multi-stage pipeline, but memory management — not raw compute — is the binding constraint Panopticus identifies.

The unresolved question is precisely how much the concurrent workload (YOLO + tracker + LiDAR + SLAM) degrades individual model latency from their isolated benchmarks. No shortlisted paper measures this compound system on the Orin Nano. The benchmarks from Alqahtani et al. (2024), Rey et al. (2025), and Pham et al. (2023) are all single-task measurements, meaning the 23 ms YOLO figure and the 22.6 FPS DR-SPAAM figure (Jia et al., 2020) cannot simply be added to produce a total system latency prediction.

### Theme 2: YOLO Family Variants Present a Genuine Speed-Accuracy Tradeoff That Cannot Be Resolved Without Empirical Testing on Target Hardware

The YOLO family has diversified rapidly, and the shortlist captures two distinct generations of this evolution. Terven and Cordova-Esparza (2023) provide the architectural reference arc from YOLOv1 through YOLOv8, tracing how successive versions incorporated anchor-free heads, decoupled detection branches, and improved training recipes. YOLOv8n — the nano variant — represents the established baseline: Alqahtani et al. (2024) measure it at 23 ms on Orin Nano INT8, and Rey et al. (2025) show it sustains over 50 FPS on the one-tier-higher Orin NX. These numbers satisfy the pipeline's detection latency budget with margin to spare.

Wang et al. (2024) propose YOLOv10 as a structural improvement over the v8/v9 lineage by eliminating NMS post-processing via consistent dual assignments. NMS removal reduces end-to-end latency beyond what the backbone alone provides — on standard GPU benchmarks, YOLOv10 achieves 46% lower latency than YOLOv9 at equivalent accuracy. This matters because NMS can add 3–10 ms on constrained hardware and represents a pure overhead cost with no algorithmic benefit once eliminated. The NeurIPS 2024 publication and strong COCO benchmark numbers give YOLOv10 strong theoretical standing as the candidate to displace YOLOv8n.

The critical gap is that no shortlisted paper reports YOLOv10 running on Jetson hardware with TensorRT. Alqahtani et al. (2024) and Rey et al. (2025) both measure YOLOv8. Wang et al. (2024) report COCO benchmarks on server GPUs. Whether YOLOv10's NMS-free design translates cleanly to TensorRT INT8 on the Orin Nano — or introduces new export friction — is an open empirical question the team must resolve. The pipeline proposal treats this as "a key decision to validate empirically," and the literature supports that framing: no published benchmark settles the YOLOv8n vs. YOLOv10n comparison on Jetson hardware. This is a directly publishable gap.

### Theme 3: Multi-Object Tracking Involves a Three-Way Tradeoff Among Speed, Occlusion Robustness, and Embedded Hardware Fit

The tracker choice has outsized consequences for the wheelchair pipeline because indoor corridors produce exactly the failure modes that differentiate trackers: partial occlusion by furniture, people stepping behind columns or other people, and re-identification after a person re-enters the frame. The shortlist covers three live candidates and one embedded-specialized system.

ByteTrack (Zhang et al., 2022) achieves 80.3 MOTA at 30 FPS on a V100 GPU by associating both high- and low-confidence detections with tracklets, recovering true objects that would otherwise be discarded as noise. Its critical advantage for the wheelchair is precisely this low-confidence association: a person partially behind a door frame generates a weak YOLO detection, and ByteTrack recovers the tracklet continuity rather than dropping and re-spawning the track. It is integrated natively into Ultralytics YOLOv8's `model.track()` API, making it the lowest-friction default. OC-SORT (Cao et al., 2023) addresses a different failure mode: Kalman filter error accumulation during occlusion intervals. By constructing a virtual trajectory from observations at the start and end of an occlusion, OC-SORT provides better re-identification after extended disappearances (a person walking behind a large piece of furniture). Its headline number — 700+ FPS on a single CPU — means it adds near-zero GPU overhead, leaving the full GPU budget for YOLO inference on the Jetson.

HopTrack (Li et al., 2024) occupies a distinct niche: it is the only candidate designed and benchmarked specifically on Jetson-class embedded hardware (AGX Xavier), achieving 39.29 FPS with 63.12% MOTA on MOT16 while reducing energy by 20.8% and memory by 8%. For a battery-powered wheelchair where thermal budget and memory capacity are both constrained, these embedded-specific optimizations have genuine value that raw benchmark numbers on server GPUs cannot capture. The tension in the literature is that ByteTrack and OC-SORT have superior MOTA numbers and stronger venues (ECCV 2022 and CVPR 2023), but neither was validated on Jetson hardware. HopTrack fills that niche but with weaker absolute accuracy. The team must decide whether the embedded hardware fit (HopTrack) or the accuracy ceiling (OC-SORT for occlusion, ByteTrack for throughput) is the binding constraint — a decision the literature leaves open.

### Theme 4: Sensor Fusion Between RGB-D and 2D LiDAR Is Empirically Justified, but Fusion Architecture Choices Remain Contested

The dual-sensor architecture — RGB-D camera for visual detection with depth, plus 2D LiDAR for floor-level obstacle coverage — is validated across multiple lines of evidence in the shortlist. Tamim et al. (2026) provide the strongest direct empirical justification: a comparative study on JRDB using camera-only (BEVDepth), LiDAR-only (PointPillars), and fusion-based (DAL) 3D person detection shows that fusion models outperform single-modality approaches in challenging conditions (occlusion, distance variation). Camera-only approaches specifically show the weakest performance under occlusion — the exact failure mode that plagues indoor wheelchair navigation when people pass through doorways or stand near furniture.

Xu et al. (2023, 2024) demonstrate the practical complementarity at the sensing level rather than the model level. Their RGB-D systems, validated on Intel RealSense D435i (the same sensor as the wheelchair project), achieve 0.11 m position error in crowded environments by separating static and dynamic obstacle detection — the static/dynamic distinction is critical for feeding a clean Nav2 costmap. Plozza et al. (2024) demonstrate the LiDAR side: their 2D LiDAR person tracker achieves 85.45% average MOTA at 20 Hz on Jetson Xavier NX in real dynamic indoor environments, directly confirming that the LiDAR layer can meet frequency and accuracy requirements. Jia et al. (2020) place DR-SPAAM as the state-of-the-art algorithm for 2D LiDAR person detection, achieving 22.6 FPS on a Jetson AGX embedded GPU with pre-trained PyTorch models available.

The fusion architecture debate is between early fusion (combining sensor data before detection) and late fusion (running independent detectors and associating their outputs). The pipeline proposal adopts late fusion via spatial proximity matching (a LiDAR detection with no RGB-D match within 0.4 m is treated as an unclassified obstacle). Tamim et al. (2026) note that their fusion model (DAL) remains sensitive to sensor misalignment, suggesting that early/tight fusion introduces calibration fragility that late fusion avoids. However, the shortlist does not contain a paper that directly compares early and late fusion latency on Jetson-class hardware for this specific sensor pair. That comparison is an open empirical question.

### Theme 5: ROS2/Nav2 Is the Established Integration Framework — but Dynamic Obstacle Handling in Its Costmap Layer Requires Careful Pipeline Design

The wheelchair's fast safety loop terminates in a Nav2 costmap that routes the path planner around detected obstacles. Macenski et al. (2023), written by the Nav2 maintainers, provide the authoritative reference for this stack. Their survey covers costmap layers, SLAM integration (SLAM Toolbox), dynamic obstacle inflation, and the MPPI local planner — every component the perception pipeline must feed into. The implicit architectural contract is that the perception pipeline publishes clean, low-latency obstacle information to Nav2's costmap layers, which then apply inflation radii and generate the navigable space representation.

The challenge is that Nav2's costmap was originally designed for static maps with sensor-update layers, not for richly structured dynamic object representations. Xu et al. (2023) address this from the pipeline side, demonstrating that separating static and dynamic obstacle detection before costmap injection preserves map quality and avoids ghost-trail artifacts from moving objects. Saha et al. (2022), validated on the same RealSense D435i sensor on Jetson TX2, achieve 0.6 ms per detection at 60 Hz using depth-image-based dynamic thresholding — demonstrating that a lightweight fallback detection layer can maintain costmap updates even when the YOLO model is resource-constrained. Toupas et al. (2023) demonstrate a complete edge-deployed pipeline in a healthcare/assisted living context — the closest operational domain match in the shortlist — that keeps all computation on-device to avoid cloud latency penalties, exactly as the wheelchair's fast loop architecture requires.

The unresolved tension here is between detection richness and Nav2 integration simplicity. The pipeline proposal's scene state message (structured JSON with per-object velocity, time-to-collision, and class label) is richer than Nav2's costmap layer API was designed to consume. Publishing rich structured state to the LLM slow loop is straightforward, but feeding velocity and class-awareness into the costmap for differentiated obstacle treatment (e.g., inflating a moving person's cell differently from a static bag) requires custom costmap layer code not documented in Macenski et al. (2023). This is a software integration gap, not a research gap, but it is a concrete implementation risk the literature does not resolve.

### Theme 6: Evaluation Datasets and Benchmarks Expose a Viewpoint Mismatch Between the Academic Baseline and the Wheelchair Use Case

The dominant benchmark datasets for indoor person detection and tracking were collected from sensor rigs at standing adult height (approximately 1.6–1.8 m), while a power wheelchair-mounted camera is at approximately 0.8–1.2 m. This viewpoint difference affects detection difficulty (more occlusion of lower bodies, different perspective foreshortening), training distribution alignment, and how published benchmark metrics transfer to wheelchair deployment. Martín-Martín et al. (2021) created JRDB specifically to address the egocentric robot viewpoint problem: data collected from JackRabbot at roughly wheelchair height, with 2.3 million bounding boxes across 3,500+ human trajectories in indoor and outdoor environments, and crucially, multimodal sensors (LiDAR + RGB-D) that match the wheelchair's sensor suite. JRDB is the only shortlisted dataset that directly addresses this viewpoint and sensor configuration.

Tamim et al. (2026) use JRDB as their evaluation benchmark, making their fusion comparison results (camera-only vs. LiDAR-only vs. fusion) directly interpretable for the wheelchair context. Zhang et al. (2022) and Cao et al. (2023) evaluate primarily on MOT17 and MOT20 — pedestrian tracking datasets collected from traffic and surveillance camera perspectives at full standing height, with very different occlusion patterns and crowd densities than wheelchair-height indoor corridors. The 80.3 MOTA ByteTrack achieves on MOT17 may not translate directly to wheelchair corridor tracking because the training distribution, viewpoint, and dominant occlusion types differ. HopTrack (Li et al., 2024) evaluates on MOT16, with the same viewpoint limitation.

The shortlist does not contain any paper that benchmarks a YOLO detector or MOT tracker on JRDB. This is a concrete research gap: the best available indoor-robot-viewpoint benchmark exists, and no shortlisted paper applies the pipeline's preferred detection and tracking components to it. Closing this gap — running YOLOv8n/v10n + ByteTrack/OC-SORT on JRDB at robot camera height — would produce directly publishable results and is achievable with the team's hardware.

## Agreements

**Real-time detection on Jetson Orin Nano is achievable with TensorRT INT8 quantization.** Alqahtani et al. (2024) report 23 ms for YOLOv8n; Rey et al. (2025) report 52–65 FPS for YOLOv8n/s; Pham et al. (2023) report 47.56 FPS end-to-end video pipeline. All three use TensorRT optimization. Without TensorRT, none of these numbers hold.

**2D LiDAR person detection is feasible at pipeline frequencies on embedded GPU.** Jia et al. (2020) report 22.6 FPS for DR-SPAAM on Jetson AGX embedded GPU. Plozza et al. (2024) demonstrate a complete LiDAR-based person tracker achieving 85.45% MOTA at 20 Hz on Jetson Xavier NX. Both confirm that the LiDAR detection layer can be sustained at the pipeline's required 20 Hz update rate.

**Fusion outperforms single-modality detection under occlusion and distance variation.** Tamim et al. (2026) empirically demonstrate fusion superiority on JRDB across multiple difficulty levels. The camera-only approach fails worst under occlusion — the dominant failure mode for indoor wheelchair navigation.

**Separating static from dynamic obstacle detection is essential for Nav2 costmap quality.** Xu et al. (2023, 2024) both emphasize static/dynamic separation as a core design principle, demonstrating that mixing stationary and moving obstacles in a single detection layer degrades map quality and produces ghost artifacts.

**ByteTrack is the default tracker for YOLO-paired pipelines.** Zhang et al. (2022) establish ByteTrack as the benchmark, and it is the tracker integrated into Ultralytics YOLOv8. No shortlisted paper proposes a superior YOLO-paired tracker with better speed-accuracy tradeoffs and equivalent integration ease. The implicit consensus across the shortlist is that ByteTrack is the starting point any alternative must beat.

**Intel RealSense D435i is a suitable and well-supported RGB-D sensor for this application.** Xu et al. (2023, 2024) both validate specifically on RealSense D435i in real robot navigation contexts, and Saha et al. (2022) validate on the same sensor on Jetson TX2. Three independent papers using this exact sensor converge on it as a reliable choice with mature ROS driver support.

**Edge-only (no-cloud) architecture is operationally required.** Toupas et al. (2023) explicitly justify keeping all pipeline stages on the edge device to eliminate cloud latency penalties — the same design requirement the wheelchair pipeline imposes on the fast loop. Rey et al. (2025) confirm that cloud-offload introduces unacceptable latency variability for real-time edge-device deployments.

## Contradictions & Debates

**YOLOv8n versus YOLOv10n on Jetson Orin Nano.** The literature presents a genuine unresolved disagreement by absence: Alqahtani et al. (2024) and Rey et al. (2025) measure YOLOv8n/s on the target hardware; Wang et al. (2024) demonstrate YOLOv10's architectural advantages (NMS elimination, 46% latency reduction over YOLOv9) exclusively on server GPUs. No shortlisted paper provides YOLOv10 latency on Jetson hardware. The team cannot resolve this from the literature and must run the comparison themselves. The disagreement is not between papers but between a strong architectural argument (NMS-free → faster) and absent empirical evidence on the target platform.

**ByteTrack versus OC-SORT for indoor corridor occlusion.** Zhang et al. (2022) optimize for high recall via low-confidence detection association — the right property when YOLO produces weak detections of partially visible people. Cao et al. (2023) optimize for error correction over long occlusion intervals via virtual trajectories — the right property when people disappear behind furniture and re-emerge. The papers' benchmark results (ByteTrack: 80.3 MOTA on MOT17; OC-SORT: state-of-the-art on MOT17/20/KITTI/DanceTrack) are not directly comparable because they are evaluated on different protocol versions and the papers optimize different metrics. In the wheelchair context, both failure modes occur. The literature does not provide a head-to-head comparison on indoor, low-camera-height scenarios. The pipeline proposal resolves this by designating ByteTrack as default and OC-SORT as fallback for occlusion-heavy scenes, but this is a design decision, not an empirically established best practice.

**Embedded-optimized tracking (HopTrack) versus accuracy-optimized tracking (ByteTrack/OC-SORT).** HopTrack (Li et al., 2024) provides 39.29 FPS on AGX Xavier with 20.8% energy savings — properties that matter for a battery-powered wheelchair. ByteTrack (Zhang et al., 2022) and OC-SORT (Cao et al., 2023) achieve better MOTA numbers but on server-class hardware. Whether the accuracy advantage of ByteTrack/OC-SORT survives on the Orin Nano when running concurrently with YOLO, DR-SPAAM, and SLAM is not established. The embedded-constraint papers (HopTrack, Panopticus) consistently show that server-measured accuracy does not translate intact to embedded deployment. This creates a genuine uncertainty: the trackers with better accuracy benchmarks may not maintain their advantage under the Orin Nano's concurrent workload.

**Fusion sensitivity to sensor misalignment.** Tamim et al. (2026) find that fusion models provide superior results in challenging conditions but that DAL (their fusion model) "remains sensitive to sensor misalignment and LiDAR-based corruptions." The pipeline proposal adopts late fusion (spatial proximity matching) precisely to avoid tight calibration dependence, but the literature does not provide a comparison of early-fusion versus late-fusion fragility under real-world calibration drift. On a power wheelchair that experiences vibration and physical impacts, calibration drift is a realistic operational concern not addressed by any shortlisted paper.

**FP16 versus INT8 quantization for detection accuracy.** The pipeline proposal explicitly flags this as a decision to validate empirically. Alqahtani et al. (2024) benchmark INT8 but do not report FP16 latency for comparison on the Orin Nano. Rey et al. (2025) compare INT8 against unquantized FP32 but do not report per-class accuracy degradation. The shortlist does not contain a paper that characterizes how INT8 quantization specifically degrades detection accuracy for partially occluded people or small objects (e.g., chair legs, bags), which are the wheelchair's most important edge-case detections.

## Open Questions & Research Gaps

**What is the compound latency of the full pipeline stack on Jetson Orin Nano?** Every shortlisted benchmark measures a single model or subsystem in isolation. The sum of isolated latency numbers (23 ms YOLO + ~5 ms ByteTrack + ~44 ms DR-SPAAM + SLAM overhead) does not account for GPU contention, memory bandwidth sharing, or interrupt scheduling. No paper characterizes the end-to-end latency of a YOLO+tracker+LiDAR detector stack running concurrently on Orin Nano hardware. This is the team's primary empirical gap and is directly publishable.

**Does DR-SPAAM meaningfully improve safety recall over RGB-D YOLO alone in wheelchair-height indoor scenarios?** The complementary coverage argument — LiDAR catches what camera misses — is supported by Tamim et al.'s (2026) fusion superiority finding and the pipeline proposal's coverage analysis, but no shortlisted paper measures ablative recall improvement (camera-only vs. camera+LiDAR) in a wheelchair-height indoor corridor scenario. Plozza et al. (2024) demonstrate LiDAR tracking works; Tamim et al. (2026) demonstrate fusion beats single modality; but the margin — how many additional safety-relevant detections LiDAR adds to what YOLO already captures — has not been measured in this operational context.

**How do YOLO and MOT trackers perform at wheelchair camera height on JRDB?** Martín-Martín et al. (2021) provide the definitive robot-viewpoint indoor benchmark, but no shortlisted paper evaluates the candidate detectors or trackers on JRDB. The known viewpoint mismatch between MOT17/20 (standing height, outdoor/surveillance) and wheelchair deployment means published accuracy numbers are not directly trustworthy. This is a concrete experimental gap.

**What is the effect of INT8 quantization on detection accuracy for safety-critical small and occluded objects?** The shortlist confirms INT8 reduces latency. No paper characterizes per-class accuracy degradation for the specific detection classes the wheelchair prioritizes (person, partially visible; low-profile obstacle). This matters because the wheelchair's safety requirements are asymmetric: a missed person detection is much more costly than a false positive.

**How should a late-fusion spatial proximity threshold be calibrated?** The pipeline proposal specifies 0.4 m as the RGB-D/LiDAR association threshold, but no shortlisted paper empirically characterizes the false positive and false negative rate of spatial proximity matching as a function of threshold value in a real indoor environment. Tamim et al. (2026) raise sensor misalignment as a fusion failure mode but do not address the threshold calibration question.

**How do detection and tracking systems degrade under low-light and cluttered indoor conditions?** Alqahtani et al. (2024) explicitly flag robustness testing under occlusion and low-light as absent from their benchmark. Rey et al. (2025) similarly do not test environmental robustness. Xu et al. (2023, 2024) test in crowded environments but do not report low-light or adversarial scene conditions. The shortlist has near-zero robustness evidence for degraded perception conditions — exactly the conditions that increase wheelchair collision risk.

**What is the minimum viable scene representation for the LLM slow loop interface?** The pipeline proposal raises this as Research Question 3, and none of the shortlisted papers address the LLM interface directly. The fast loop's scene state message design (structured JSON with per-object position, velocity, and time-to-collision) is proposed without empirical validation that this representation is sufficient or optimal for LLM-based navigation intent. This is joint work with the LLM teammates but is identified as a gap in the perception pipeline literature.

**How does tracking stability degrade as crowd density increases?** ByteTrack (Zhang et al., 2022) and OC-SORT (Cao et al., 2023) are evaluated on crowd tracking benchmarks (MOT17, MOT20), but the wheelchair's indoor corridor scenarios involve 1–5 people rather than dense pedestrian crowds. Performance at low pedestrian density (where individual track continuity matters more than aggregate MOTA) is not a primary focus of any shortlisted tracker paper.

## Methodological Notes

**Dominant methods in the shortlist:** Hardware benchmark studies (Alqahtani et al., 2024; Rey et al., 2025; Pham et al., 2023) measure latency and throughput for defined models on defined hardware; standard evaluation is against COCO mAP for detection and MOTA/IDF1 on MOT datasets for tracking. Algorithm papers (Zhang et al., 2022; Cao et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024) follow benchmark-comparison methodology, reporting improvements on established leaderboards. System papers (Plozza et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2023, 2024; Toupas et al., 2023) validate full pipelines on physical hardware in real or semi-controlled environments. Dataset papers (Martín-Martín et al., 2021) define evaluation protocols and ground-truth annotations.

**Hardware generation gaps:** DR-SPAAM (Jia et al., 2020) is validated on Jetson AGX; Plozza et al. (2024) on Jetson Xavier NX; HopTrack (Li et al., 2024) on Jetson AGX Xavier; Pham et al. (2023) on Jetson Orin Nano; Alqahtani et al. (2024) and Lee et al. (2024) on Jetson Orin Nano. There is no consistent hardware baseline across the shortlist, making cross-paper latency comparisons approximate. The team must normalize all numbers to Orin Nano performance through empirical testing.

**Detection benchmark mismatch:** Trackers are evaluated on MOT17/20/16 (surveillance or autonomous vehicle viewpoint, standing adult height). Detectors are evaluated on COCO (diverse viewpoints, mixed indoor/outdoor). The only robot-egocentric benchmark is JRDB (Martín-Martín et al., 2021), but none of the shortlisted model papers report JRDB results. Published accuracy numbers are therefore not directly comparable to wheelchair deployment performance.

**Venue quality range:** The shortlist spans top-tier venues (CVPR 2023, ECCV 2022, NeurIPS 2024, TPAMI 2021, MobiCom 2024, RA-L 2024, ICRA 2023, IROS 2020) through solid mid-tier venues (IEEE Sensors Applications Symposium, MDPI Sensors, MDPI Electronics, ICIP 2024) and preprints (Alqahtani et al., 2024; Toupas et al., 2023; HopTrack, Li et al., 2024). The preprints provide the highest topical relevance (edge deployment of YOLO on Orin Nano) but the lowest confidence in results.

**Geographic and operational environment gap:** No shortlisted paper evaluates perception in an actual wheelchair deployment or in the specific environment types wheelchairs traverse (hospital corridors, assisted living facilities, home interiors with low furniture and cluttered floors). The closest is Toupas et al. (2023) in a healthcare/assisted living context, but without quantitative latency numbers in the abstract. The operational domain transfer from the shortlisted evidence base to the wheelchair use case is an inference, not a validated fact.

**Energy consumption as an underreported metric:** Rey et al. (2025) and Pham et al. (2023) both report energy per inference or total power draw, but most shortlisted papers do not. For a battery-powered wheelchair where autonomy directly affects usability, energy consumption is a first-class system constraint that the academic benchmark literature systematically underreports.

## Recommended Narrative Arc for Report

The report should be structured to move from the hardware feasibility question through the algorithmic component choices to the sensor fusion justification, and close with the gap analysis that motivates the team's specific experimental contributions.

1. **Open with the hardware constraint as the governing design decision.** Establish that Jetson Orin Nano (~20 TOPS INT8) is the execution target, and that every downstream algorithmic choice is evaluated against the 50 ms fast loop latency budget. Cite Alqahtani et al. (2024) and Rey et al. (2025) as the primary evidence that TensorRT-optimized YOLO meets this budget, and Panopticus (Lee et al., 2024) and Pham et al. (2023) as evidence that multi-task concurrent deployment on Orin Nano is feasible but requires memory-aware system design.

2. **Introduce the YOLO detection layer as the pipeline core.** Use Terven and Cordova-Esparza (2023) to establish the YOLO architectural lineage and justify the nano/small variant selection. Present the YOLOv8n baseline numbers (Alqahtani et al., 2024; Rey et al., 2025) and the theoretical case for YOLOv10 (Wang et al., 2024), framing the comparison as an open empirical question the team will resolve.

3. **Cover the multi-object tracking layer as an architectural choice under competing constraints.** Introduce ByteTrack (Zhang et al., 2022) as the integration-friction-minimizing default, OC-SORT (Cao et al., 2023) as the occlusion-robust alternative with near-zero GPU cost, and HopTrack (Li et al., 2024) as the embedded-optimized option. Present the three-way tradeoff explicitly rather than recommending a single winner, and frame the ByteTrack vs. OC-SORT corridor comparison as a team experimental contribution.

4. **Justify the dual-sensor architecture through the fusion evidence.** Lead with Tamim et al. (2026) on fusion superiority under occlusion, then move through DR-SPAAM (Jia et al., 2020) and Plozza et al. (2024) to establish the LiDAR person detection layer's feasibility, and Xu et al. (2023, 2024) and Saha et al. (2022) to establish the RGB-D detection layer. Frame late fusion as the pragmatic choice given sensor misalignment sensitivity.

5. **Situate the pipeline within the ROS2/Nav2 integration context.** Use Macenski et al. (2023) as the integration reference, and Xu et al. (2023) to explain the static/dynamic separation requirement for costmap quality. Briefly characterize the scene state publisher as the interface between the pipeline and both the fast loop (Nav2 costmap) and the slow loop (LLM teammates).

6. **Close with the evaluation and gap section.** Use Martín-Martín et al. (2021) to establish JRDB as the right evaluation benchmark and identify the absence of JRDB-evaluated YOLO/MOT results as the literature's central gap for the wheelchair use case. Enumerate the team's open research questions (YOLOv8n vs. v10n on Orin Nano, ByteTrack vs. OC-SORT in corridors, ablative LiDAR contribution to recall, compound pipeline latency) as directly publishable contributions motivated by this synthesis.
