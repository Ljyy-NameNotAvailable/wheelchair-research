# Real-Time Indoor Perception Pipeline for Shared Autonomy Wheelchair: YOLO, Multi-Object Tracking, Sensor Fusion, and Edge Deployment

**Literature Review**
**Date:** 2026-04-11
**Topic:** Indoor wheelchair perception pipeline — YOLO-based detection, multi-object tracking, RGB-D + 2D LiDAR sensor fusion, and edge deployment on NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano

---

## Abstract

This literature review examines the research foundations for a real-time indoor perception pipeline designed to support shared autonomy in a power wheelchair deployed on an NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano (~20 TOPS INT8). Eighteen papers were shortlisted from a broader candidate pool, covering edge deployment benchmarks, YOLO detector variants, multi-object tracking algorithms, RGB-D and 2D LiDAR sensing, sensor fusion methodology, ROS2/Nav2 integration, and evaluation datasets. The review finds that TensorRT INT8-optimized YOLO inference is feasible within a 50 ms safety loop budget (Alqahtani et al., 2024; Rey et al., 2025), that sensor fusion outperforms single-modality detection under occlusion (Tamim et al., 2026), and that ByteTrack and OC-SORT represent complementary tracking strategies suited to different indoor failure modes (Zhang et al., 2022; Cao et al., 2023). Key gaps identified include: the absence of any compound pipeline latency measurement running YOLO, tracking, LiDAR detection, and SLAM concurrently on the Orin Nano; no published benchmark applying the candidate detectors or trackers to the JRDB robot-viewpoint dataset (Martín-Martín et al., 2021); and uncharacterized INT8 accuracy degradation for partially occluded and low-profile obstacles. These gaps represent directly publishable experimental contributions for the project team.

## Themes

### Theme 1: The Jetson Orin Nano Is a Viable but Constrained Execution Target — If TensorRT Optimization Is Applied

The governing design constraint for the entire perception pipeline is whether real-time inference is achievable within the computational envelope of the NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano, rated at approximately 20 TOPS INT8. The shortlisted benchmarks converge on a qualified affirmative, contingent on disciplined application of TensorRT optimization. Alqahtani et al. (2024) provide the most direct validation for the wheelchair use case: YOLOv8n achieves 23 ms inference latency with INT8 quantization on Jetson Orin Nano, a figure that satisfies the pipeline's 50 ms safety loop budget with margin remaining for tracker, LiDAR, and scene state publisher overhead. Rey et al. (2025) extend this result by benchmarking YOLOv8n/s on the one-tier-higher Jetson Orin NX at 52–65 FPS with TensorRT INT8, establishing the performance ceiling while also demonstrating that Raspberry Pi 5 fails to meet real-time requirements entirely — a finding that confines viable edge hardware for this class of pipeline to the Jetson product line.

These single-model figures, however, must be interpreted in the context of a multi-task concurrent workload. The wheelchair pipeline must sustain YOLO detection, multi-object tracking, DR-SPAAM LiDAR person detection, and SLAM simultaneously. Lee et al. (2024) address this multi-task reality directly: their Panopticus system, tested explicitly on the Jetson Orin Nano 6.3 GB model, uses an adaptive multi-branch detection scheme that dynamically sheds model branches under resource pressure to maintain a 33 ms latency target while improving accuracy by 62% over fixed-architecture baselines. The adaptive shedding strategy — reducing model complexity in real time when concurrent workloads saturate memory bandwidth — represents a principled response to the binding constraint that Lee et al. (2024) identify as memory capacity rather than raw compute throughput. Pham et al. (2023) add a cross-generational perspective: the Orin Nano achieves 47.56 FPS on a full video-analysis pipeline using only 3.11 GB RAM with TorchTensorRT, while delivering 15% better throughput and 50% lower energy consumption than the prior Jetson Nano generation. This headroom suggests meaningful compute budget for the wheelchair's multi-stage pipeline, but the memory ceiling remains the binding constraint that system design must address before any individual model's benchmark numbers can be treated as additive.

The critical unresolved question is how much the compound workload degrades individual model latency from their isolated benchmarks. Alqahtani et al. (2024), Rey et al. (2025), and Pham et al. (2023) all measure single-task performance. No shortlisted paper characterizes the end-to-end latency of YOLO, a tracker, and a LiDAR detector running concurrently on Orin Nano hardware, meaning the per-component numbers cannot be summed to predict total system latency. Measuring this compound latency is the team's primary empirical contribution and is directly publishable.

### Theme 2: YOLO Family Variants Present a Genuine Speed-Accuracy Tradeoff That Cannot Be Resolved Without Empirical Testing on Target Hardware

The YOLO family has diversified substantially, and the shortlist captures two generationally distinct candidates for the wheelchair pipeline's detection layer. Terven and Cordova-Esparza (2023) provide the architectural reference spanning YOLOv1 through YOLOv8, documenting the progression from anchor-based to anchor-free detection heads, the introduction of decoupled classification and regression branches, and improved training recipes that collectively define the modern YOLO design space. YOLOv8n — the nano variant — represents the established, hardware-validated baseline for this pipeline: Alqahtani et al. (2024) measure it at 23 ms on the Orin Nano with INT8 quantization, and Rey et al. (2025) show it sustains over 50 FPS on the Orin NX. These numbers satisfy the pipeline's detection latency budget with margin that the tracker and LiDAR layers must then consume.

Wang et al. (2024) introduce YOLOv10 as a structural advancement over the v8/v9 lineage. The defining contribution is the elimination of non-maximum suppression post-processing through consistent dual assignments, replacing a component that adds 3–10 ms on constrained hardware with a pure overhead cost that has no information-theoretic justification once the dual-assignment training objective is applied. On standard GPU benchmarks, YOLOv10 achieves 46% lower latency than YOLOv9 at equivalent COCO accuracy, and its NeurIPS 2024 publication provides institutional credibility. The architectural argument — NMS elimination reduces end-to-end latency without accuracy sacrifice — makes YOLOv10n a strong theoretical candidate to displace YOLOv8n as the pipeline's detection backbone.

The critical gap is that Wang et al. (2024) report all performance figures on server-class GPUs, not on Jetson hardware with TensorRT INT8. Whether YOLOv10's NMS-free design exports cleanly to TensorRT's optimization graph, or introduces new friction in the INT8 quantization process, is an empirical question that no shortlisted paper answers. This is not a theoretical uncertainty resolvable by extrapolation from existing benchmarks — it must be measured on the target hardware. The literature therefore supports treating the YOLOv8n versus YOLOv10n comparison as an open experimental question that constitutes a directly publishable benchmark study for the team.

### Theme 3: Multi-Object Tracking Involves a Three-Way Tradeoff Among Speed, Occlusion Robustness, and Embedded Hardware Fit

The tracker choice has outsized consequences for the wheelchair pipeline because the dominant failure modes of indoor corridor navigation — partial occlusion by doorframes, furniture, and other people, and re-identification after a person re-enters the frame — are precisely the scenarios that differentiate tracker algorithms from one another. The shortlist provides three live candidates representing distinct design philosophies.

ByteTrack (Zhang et al., 2022) reframes the tracking problem around the low-confidence detection regime. Rather than discarding detections below a confidence threshold, ByteTrack associates both high- and low-confidence detection boxes with existing tracklets, recovering true objects that would otherwise be treated as background noise. This strategy is particularly important for the wheelchair: a person partially occluded by a doorframe generates a weak YOLO detection, and ByteTrack recovers tracklet continuity rather than dropping and re-spawning the identity. The algorithm achieves 80.3 MOTA, 77.3 IDF1, and 63.1 HOTA on MOT17 at 30 FPS on a V100 GPU, and it is integrated natively into the Ultralytics YOLOv8 API through a single `model.track()` call — the lowest integration-friction option available. OC-SORT (Cao et al., 2023) addresses a complementary failure mode: Kalman filter error accumulation during extended occlusion intervals. By constructing a virtual trajectory from the detector observations at the start and end of an occlusion interval, OC-SORT corrects the propagated state error that degrades re-identification after a person walks behind a large piece of furniture. Its headline performance of 700+ FPS on a single CPU means it adds near-zero GPU overhead on the Jetson, leaving the full GPU budget for YOLO inference. OC-SORT achieves state-of-the-art results on MOT17, MOT20, KITTI, and DanceTrack — the most comprehensive multi-benchmark validation in the candidate pool.

HopTrack (Li et al., 2024) occupies a distinct niche that neither ByteTrack nor OC-SORT addresses: it is the only candidate designed and benchmarked specifically on Jetson-class embedded hardware. On Jetson AGX Xavier, HopTrack achieves 39.29 FPS with 63.12% MOTA on MOT16, while reducing energy consumption by 20.8%, power draw by 5%, and memory usage by 8% relative to conventional tracker implementations. For a battery-powered wheelchair where thermal budget and 8 GB memory capacity are both constrained, these embedded-specific optimizations have genuine operational value that server-GPU benchmark numbers cannot capture. The fundamental tension is that ByteTrack and OC-SORT have superior MOTA figures and stronger publication venues (ECCV 2022 and CVPR 2023 respectively), but neither was validated on Jetson hardware under concurrent workloads. HopTrack fills this embedded niche but with weaker absolute accuracy metrics. The literature does not provide a head-to-head comparison of these three candidates on indoor, wheelchair-height scenarios, leaving the tracker selection as an empirically open decision.

### Theme 4: Sensor Fusion Between RGB-D and 2D LiDAR Is Empirically Justified, but Fusion Architecture Choices Remain Contested

The dual-sensor architecture — Intel RealSense D435i for RGB-D visual detection with depth, plus 2D LiDAR for floor-level obstacle coverage — is validated across multiple independent lines of evidence in the shortlist. Tamim et al. (2026) provide the strongest direct empirical justification. Using the JRDB benchmark across varying occlusion and distance levels, they compare camera-only (BEVDepth), LiDAR-only (PointPillars), and fusion-based (DAL) 3D person detection and find that fusion models provide superior results across challenging conditions. Critically, the camera-only approach shows the weakest performance specifically under occlusion — the dominant failure mode of indoor wheelchair navigation when people pass through doorways or stand partially behind furniture. This finding moves the dual-sensor architecture from engineering intuition to empirical justification on the most topically relevant benchmark available.

The LiDAR detection layer is independently validated at the algorithmic and system levels. Jia et al. (2020) establish DR-SPAAM as the state-of-the-art algorithm for 2D LiDAR person detection in the range data domain, achieving 22.6 FPS on a Jetson AGX embedded GPU with a PyTorch implementation and pre-trained models released openly. The forward-looking autoregressive architecture avoids expensive multi-scan alignment while maintaining detection accuracy comparable to methods using richer input representations. Plozza et al. (2024) provide system-level validation: their complete 2D LiDAR person tracking pipeline achieves 85.45% average MOTA at 20 Hz on a Jetson Xavier NX in real-world dynamic indoor environments, with validated integration into a navigation planner on a quadruped robot — the closest physical analogue to the wheelchair's fast loop architecture in the shortlist. Together, these papers confirm that the LiDAR detection layer can sustain the pipeline's required 20 Hz update rate on Jetson-class hardware.

The RGB-D sensing layer is similarly grounded. Xu et al. (2023) demonstrate real-time dynamic obstacle detection, tracking, and mapping using the Intel RealSense D435i — the wheelchair project's exact sensor — in crowded environments on resource-constrained UAV hardware with a ROS-integrated open-source package. Xu et al. (2024) extend this with an ensemble detection strategy that achieves 0.11 m position error and 0.23 m/s velocity error, directly applicable to the wheelchair's scene state publisher requirements. Saha et al. (2022), also validated on the RealSense D435i on a Jetson TX2, achieve 0.6 ms per detection at 60 Hz using depth-image-based dynamic thresholding — demonstrating a computationally trivial fallback detection layer capable of maintaining costmap updates when YOLO is resource-constrained. The critical design point arising from both Xu et al. (2023, 2024) is that separating static from dynamic obstacle detection before costmap injection is essential for maintaining map quality and avoiding ghost-trail artifacts from moving objects.

The fusion architecture debate concerns whether sensors should be integrated before detection (early fusion) or after independent detection stages are run (late fusion via spatial proximity association). Tamim et al. (2026) note that their fusion model remains sensitive to sensor misalignment, suggesting that tight early fusion introduces calibration fragility that is problematic on a physical wheelchair subject to vibration and impact. The pipeline proposal adopts late fusion — associating LiDAR detections with RGB-D tracked objects by spatial proximity in the floor plane, treating any LiDAR detection without an RGB-D match within 0.4 m as an unclassified obstacle. The shortlist does not, however, contain a paper that directly compares early and late fusion latency and robustness on Jetson-class hardware for this specific sensor pair, leaving the threshold calibration question open.

### Theme 5: ROS2/Nav2 Is the Established Integration Framework — but Dynamic Obstacle Handling in Its Costmap Layer Requires Careful Pipeline Design

The wheelchair's fast safety loop terminates in a Nav2 costmap that routes the path planner around detected obstacles, making the ROS2/Nav2 integration layer a first-class architectural concern for the perception pipeline. Macenski et al. (2023), written by the actual Nav2 maintainers, provide the authoritative technical reference for this stack, covering Nav2 planners (Hybrid A*, Smac, MPPI), costmap layers including dynamic obstacle inflation, SLAM Toolbox integration, and the MPPI local planner. Every component the perception pipeline must feed into is addressed, establishing the implicit architectural contract: the perception pipeline publishes clean, low-latency obstacle information to Nav2 costmap layers, which apply inflation radii and generate the navigable space representation for the path planner.

The challenge is that Nav2's costmap was originally designed for static map updates with sensor measurement layers, not for richly structured dynamic object representations that include per-object velocity vectors and time-to-collision estimates. Xu et al. (2023) address this from the pipeline design side, demonstrating that separating static and dynamic obstacle detection before costmap injection preserves map quality and prevents the ghost-trail artifacts that arise when moving objects are merged with the static map layer. Saha et al. (2022), validated on the RealSense D435i on a Jetson TX2, demonstrate that a lightweight depth-thresholding detection layer running at 60 Hz can serve as a safety fallback that maintains costmap updates even when the primary YOLO model is resource-saturated — a defense-in-depth architecture directly applicable to the wheelchair. Toupas et al. (2023) demonstrate a complete edge-deployed human detection, tracking, and action recognition pipeline for mobile service robots in healthcare and assisted living environments, keeping all pipeline stages on-device to eliminate cloud latency penalties and demonstrating the operational feasibility of this architecture in the wheelchair's closest documented application domain.

The unresolved tension lies between detection richness and Nav2 integration simplicity. The pipeline's scene state message — structured JSON including per-object position, velocity, time-to-collision, and class label — is richer than Nav2's costmap layer API was designed to consume directly. Publishing this rich representation to the LLM slow loop is architecturally clean, but using velocity and class-awareness to implement differentiated costmap treatment (inflating a moving person's cell differently from a stationary bag) requires custom costmap layer code that Macenski et al. (2023) do not document. This is a software engineering challenge rather than a research gap, but it represents a concrete implementation risk that the literature does not resolve.

### Theme 6: Evaluation Datasets and Benchmarks Expose a Viewpoint Mismatch Between the Academic Baseline and the Wheelchair Use Case

The dominant benchmark datasets for indoor person detection and tracking were collected from sensors mounted at standing adult height (approximately 1.6–1.8 m), while a power wheelchair-mounted camera operates at approximately 0.8–1.2 m. This viewpoint difference affects detection difficulty through altered perspective foreshortening, different occlusion pattern distribution, and training data mismatch between published models and wheelchair deployment. Martín-Martín et al. (2021) created JRDB specifically to address the egocentric robot viewpoint problem: data collected from JackRabbot at roughly wheelchair height, comprising 64 minutes of annotated multimodal sensor data with 2.3 million bounding boxes and 1.8 million 3D cuboids across over 3,500 human trajectories in indoor and outdoor environments. JRDB includes synchronized LiDAR, RGB-D, and stereo camera streams, mirroring the wheelchair project's sensor configuration. It is the only shortlisted dataset that directly addresses both the viewpoint and the sensor configuration of the target deployment.

Tamim et al. (2026) use JRDB as their evaluation benchmark, making their camera-only versus LiDAR-only versus fusion comparison results directly interpretable for the wheelchair context. By contrast, Zhang et al. (2022) and Cao et al. (2023) evaluate primarily on MOT17 and MOT20 — pedestrian tracking datasets collected from traffic and surveillance camera perspectives at full standing height, with different occlusion patterns and crowd densities than wheelchair-height indoor corridors. The 80.3 MOTA ByteTrack achieves on MOT17 cannot be transferred directly to wheelchair corridor tracking because the training distribution, dominant viewpoint, and occlusion geometry differ. HopTrack (Li et al., 2024) evaluates on MOT16, which carries the same viewpoint limitation. Alqahtani et al. (2024) benchmark on COCO, which provides mixed viewpoint and scene diversity but does not target the low-camera-height indoor person detection regime. Rey et al. (2025) similarly evaluate on COCO without indoor robot viewpoint conditions.

The concrete research gap is that no shortlisted paper benchmarks any YOLO detector or MOT tracker on JRDB. The best available indoor robot-viewpoint benchmark exists, but the pipeline's candidate components have not been applied to it. Closing this gap — evaluating YOLOv8n/v10n and ByteTrack/OC-SORT on JRDB at robot camera height — would produce accuracy numbers that are directly trustworthy for the wheelchair deployment context, as opposed to the inferred generalizability of numbers from standing-height surveillance datasets. This experiment is achievable with the team's hardware and JRDB's publicly available annotations, and it constitutes a directly publishable contribution.

## Key Papers

**Alqahtani, D. K., Cheema, A., & Toosi, A. N. (2024)** — [Benchmarking Deep Learning Models for Object Detection on Edge Computing Devices](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.16808)
*arXiv preprint arXiv:2409.16808, 2024*
This paper evaluates YOLOv8 (Nano, Small, Medium), EfficientDet Lite, and SSD across Raspberry Pi variants and Jetson Orin Nano devices, measuring inference latency, power consumption, and mAP. The central finding for this project is that YOLOv8n achieves 23 ms latency with INT8 quantization on the Jetson Orin Nano — directly validating the pipeline's 50 ms safety loop budget. The paper is the single most directly applicable hardware benchmark in the shortlist, though it measures only single-task workloads and does not test occlusion robustness or indoor-specific scenarios.

**Cao, J., Pang, J., Weng, X., Khirodkar, R., & Kitani, K. (2023)** — [Observation-Centric SORT: Rethinking SORT for Robust Multi-Object Tracking](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.14360)
*IEEE/CVF Conference on Computer Vision and Pattern Recognition (CVPR) 2023*
OC-SORT corrects the Kalman filter error accumulation that degrades tracking quality during occlusion by constructing a virtual trajectory from detector observations at the start and end of each occlusion interval. Running at 700+ FPS on a single CPU, OC-SORT adds near-zero GPU overhead on the Jetson, preserving the full GPU budget for YOLO inference. For indoor wheelchair navigation, its explicit occlusion correction addresses the failure mode of a person disappearing behind furniture and re-emerging — a scenario that standard Kalman filtering handles poorly after extended disappearances.

**Feng, C., Zhang, C., Chen, Z., Hu, W., & Ge, L. (2024)** — [Real-time Monocular Depth Estimation on Embedded Systems](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.10569)
*IEEE International Conference on Image Processing (ICIP) 2024*
This paper presents RT-MonoDepth and RT-MonoDepth-S, lightweight monocular depth estimation networks achieving 18–31 FPS on Jetson Nano and 253–364 FPS on Jetson AGX Orin at 640×192 resolution. These numbers confirm that monocular depth estimation is computationally viable as a fallback depth source on Orin-class hardware when the RealSense D435i produces noisy or incomplete depth maps. For the wheelchair pipeline, RT-MonoDepth represents a viable contingency option for Stage 4 depth lookup when active stereo depth is unavailable.

**Jia, D., Hermans, A., & Leibe, B. (2020)** — [DR-SPAAM: A Spatial-Attention and Auto-regressive Model for Person Detection in 2D Range Data](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.14079)
*IEEE/RSJ International Conference on Intelligent Robots and Systems (IROS) 2020*
DR-SPAAM uses a forward-looking autoregressive architecture that maintains intermediate backbone features as a template and recurrently updates them with new scans, avoiding expensive multi-scan alignment while achieving state-of-the-art accuracy on the DROW 2D LiDAR person detection benchmark. The algorithm runs at 22.6 FPS on a Jetson AGX embedded GPU, directly meeting the wheelchair pipeline's 20 Hz LiDAR update requirement. PyTorch implementation and pre-trained models are publicly released, making this a plug-in component for the pipeline's LiDAR detection layer.

**Lee, J., Jung, C., Kim, J., & Cha, H. (2024)** — [Panopticus: Omnidirectional 3D Object Detection on Resource-constrained Edge Devices](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.01270)
*ACM International Conference on Mobile Computing and Networking (MobiCom) 2024*
Panopticus introduces an adaptive multi-branch detection scheme that dynamically adjusts model architecture based on available hardware resources and spatial complexity, achieving a 33 ms latency target on the Jetson Orin Nano 6.3 GB model by shedding large model branches under memory pressure. It improves detection accuracy by 62% on average compared to fixed-architecture baselines while maintaining the latency constraint. For the wheelchair pipeline, Panopticus establishes that adaptive resource management — not static model selection — is the appropriate design response to concurrent multi-task workloads on the Orin Nano.

**Li, X., Chen, C., Lou, Y., Abdallah, M., Kim, K. T., & Bagchi, S. (2024)** — [HopTrack: A Real-time Multi-Object Tracking System for Embedded Devices](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00608)
*arXiv preprint arXiv:2411.00608, 2024*
HopTrack is the only tracker in the shortlist designed and benchmarked specifically for Jetson-class embedded hardware, achieving 39.29 FPS with 63.12% MOTA on MOT16 on a Jetson AGX Xavier while reducing energy consumption by 20.8%, power draw by 5%, and memory usage by 8%. Its detector-agnostic architecture allows pairing with any YOLO variant. For a battery-powered wheelchair where both thermal envelope and the Orin Nano's 8 GB memory ceiling are operational constraints, HopTrack's embedded-specific optimizations provide value that server-GPU benchmark numbers from ByteTrack and OC-SORT do not capture.

**Macenski, S., Moore, T., Lu, D., Merzlyakov, A., & Ferguson, M. (2023)** — [From the Desks of ROS Maintainers: A Survey of Modern & Capable Mobile Robotics Algorithms in the Robot Operating System 2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.15236)
*Robotics and Autonomous Systems, Vol. 168, 2023*
Written by the Nav2 maintainers themselves, this survey covers Nav2 planners (Hybrid A*, Smac, MPPI), costmap layers, dynamic obstacle inflation, and SLAM Toolbox integration — every component of the ROS2 navigation stack that the wheelchair's perception pipeline must feed. It is the authoritative architectural reference for understanding the implicit contract between the perception system and the costmap consumer, and is the essential citation for any team implementing Nav2-based shared autonomy.

**Martín-Martín, R., Patel, M., Rezatofighi, H., Shenoi, A., Gwak, J., Frankel, E., Sadeghian, A., & Savarese, S. (2021)** — [JRDB: A Dataset and Benchmark of Egocentric Robot Visual Perception of Humans in Built Environments](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.11792)
*IEEE Transactions on Pattern Analysis and Machine Intelligence (TPAMI), 2021*
JRDB provides 64 minutes of annotated multimodal sensor data — 360° stereo RGB video, LiDAR point clouds, RGB-D footage — collected from JackRabbot at approximately wheelchair height, with 2.3 million bounding boxes and 1.8 million 3D cuboids across 3,500+ human trajectories in indoor and outdoor environments. As the only dataset in the shortlist collected from a robot egocentric viewpoint at wheelchair height with synchronized LiDAR and RGB-D streams, it is the correct evaluation benchmark for the pipeline's target deployment context. No shortlisted model paper evaluates on JRDB, defining the most concrete research gap in the literature.

**Pham, H. V., Tran, T. G., Le, C. D., Le, A. D., & Vo, H. B. (2023)** — [Benchmarking Jetson Edge Devices with an End-to-End Video-Based Anomaly Detection System](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.16834)
*Future of Information and Communication Conference (FICC) 2024*
This paper deploys a video-based anomaly detection pipeline across Jetson Nano, AGX Xavier, and Orin Nano, achieving 47.56 FPS on the Orin Nano with only 3.11 GB RAM using TorchTensorRT. The Orin Nano delivers 15% better throughput and 50% lower energy consumption than the predecessor Jetson Nano generation. For the wheelchair project, the 3.11 GB RAM figure for a full multi-stage video pipeline demonstrates that the Orin Nano's 8 GB ceiling is not the limiting factor for a well-optimized pipeline, while the energy comparison quantifies the hardware generation advantage.

**Plozza, D., Marty, S., Scherrer, C., Schwartz, S., Zihlmann, S., & Magno, M. (2024)** — [Autonomous Navigation in Dynamic Human Environments with an Embedded 2D LiDAR-based Person Tracker](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.15000)
*IEEE Sensors Applications Symposium (SAS) 2024*
This paper presents a complete real-time 2D LiDAR person detection and tracking system integrated with navigation planning on a quadruped robot, achieving 85.45% average MOTA at 20 Hz on a Jetson Xavier NX in real-world dynamic indoor environments. It is the highest-scoring paper in the shortlist evaluation and the closest operational analogue to the wheelchair's LiDAR detection layer: same hardware class, same 2D LiDAR sensing modality, same indoor dynamic environment, same integration with a navigation planner. The validated 20 Hz rate directly confirms that the LiDAR side of the wheelchair's perception pipeline is achievable at the required update frequency.

**Rey, L., Bernardos, A. M., Dobrzycki, A. D., Carramiñana, D., Bergesio, L., Besada, J. A., & Casar, J. R. (2025)** — [A Performance Analysis of You Only Look Once Models for Deployment on Constrained Computational Edge Devices in Drone Applications](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.15737)
*Electronics (MDPI), Vol. 14, Issue 3, Article 638, 2025*
This peer-reviewed study benchmarks YOLOv8n and YOLOv8s on Jetson Orin Nano, Orin NX, and Raspberry Pi 5 with TensorRT INT8 quantization, reporting 52–65 FPS for YOLOv8n on the Orin NX and documenting the energy consumption profile for edge versus cloud offloading. The Raspberry Pi 5's failure to meet real-time requirements constrains viable hardware to the Jetson line for this class of pipeline. The paper's energy consumption data is a valuable secondary metric for a battery-powered wheelchair where runtime directly affects usability.

**Saha, A., Dhara, B. C., Umer, S., Kulakov, Y., Alanazi, J. M., & AlZubi, A. A. (2022)** — [Efficient Obstacle Detection and Tracking Using RGB-D Sensor Data in Dynamic Environments for Robotic Applications](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9460380/)
*Sensors (Basel), 22(17), 6537, 2022*
This paper demonstrates depth-image-based obstacle detection using a u-depth map with dynamic thresholding, validated on the Intel RealSense D435i sensor on a Jetson TX2, achieving 0.6 ms per detection at 60 Hz. The approach provides a lightweight, non-learning fallback detection layer that can maintain costmap updates when YOLO inference is resource-saturated. For the wheelchair pipeline, this establishes a practical defense-in-depth strategy where a computationally trivial detector sustains safety even under peak computational load.

**Tamim, M., Matic-Flierl, A., & Roscher, K. (2026)** — [A Comparative Study of 3D Person Detection: Sensor Modalities and Robustness in Diverse Indoor and Outdoor Environments](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05538)
*International Conference on Computer Vision Theory and Applications (VISAPP) 2026*
Using the JRDB benchmark, this paper compares camera-only (BEVDepth), LiDAR-only (PointPillars), and fusion-based (DAL) 3D person detection across varying occlusion levels and distances in indoor and outdoor scenes. Fusion models outperform both single-modality approaches in challenging conditions, while camera-only detection shows the weakest performance specifically under occlusion. This finding provides the empirical justification for the wheelchair pipeline's dual-sensor RGB-D + LiDAR architecture, and the paper's identification of sensor misalignment sensitivity in tight fusion informs the choice of late fusion via spatial proximity matching.

**Terven, J., & Cordova-Esparza, D. (2023)** — [A Comprehensive Review of YOLO Architectures in Computer Vision: From YOLOv1 to YOLOv8 and YOLO-NAS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.00501)
*Machine Learning and Knowledge Extraction (MDPI), Vol. 5, No. 4, 2023*
This 36-page review traces the full YOLO architectural lineage from v1 through v8 and YOLO-NAS, documenting the shift from anchor-based to anchor-free detection, the introduction of decoupled classification/regression heads, and the evolution of training recipes that define the modern YOLO design space. It is the canonical definitional reference for any paper selecting among YOLO variants or comparing YOLO to alternative detectors. Its limitation — no edge benchmark numbers — means it must be paired with empirical papers like Alqahtani et al. (2024) and Rey et al. (2025) to justify specific variant choices.

**Wang, A., Chen, H., Liu, L., Chen, K., Lin, Z., Han, J., & Ding, G. (2024)** — [YOLOv10: Real-Time End-to-End Object Detection](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.14458)
*NeurIPS 2024*
YOLOv10 eliminates non-maximum suppression post-processing through consistent dual assignments for NMS-free training, reducing end-to-end latency by 46% compared to YOLOv9 at equivalent COCO accuracy and outperforming RT-DETR-R18 while remaining 1.8x faster at similar accuracy. The NMS elimination removes 3–10 ms of pure overhead on constrained hardware, making YOLOv10n a strong candidate to replace YOLOv8n in the wheelchair pipeline. The critical limitation is that all benchmarks are on server-class GPUs; TensorRT INT8 performance on the Jetson Orin Nano has not been published.

**Xu, Z., Zhan, X., Chen, B., Xiu, Y., Yang, C., & Shimada, K. (2023)** — [A Real-Time Dynamic Obstacle Tracking and Mapping System for UAV Navigation and Collision Avoidance with an RGB-D Camera](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.08258)
*IEEE International Conference on Robotics and Automation (ICRA) 2023*
This ICRA paper demonstrates real-time dynamic obstacle detection, tracking, and mapping on resource-constrained UAV hardware using the Intel RealSense D435i, the wheelchair project's exact sensor. The system combines occupancy voxel mapping with Kalman and continuity filters and Markov chain trajectory prediction, separating static from dynamic obstacles before costmap injection to maintain map quality. The open-source ROS package is directly reusable by the wheelchair team, and the static/dynamic separation principle is a core design requirement for the Nav2 costmap integration.

**Zhang, Y., Sun, P., Jiang, Y., Yu, D., Weng, F., Yuan, Z., Luo, P., Liu, W., & Wang, X. (2022)** — [ByteTrack: Multi-Object Tracking by Associating Every Detection Box](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.06864)
*European Conference on Computer Vision (ECCV) 2022*
ByteTrack associates both high- and low-confidence detection boxes with existing tracklets, recovering partially visible objects that threshold-based filtering would discard. On MOT17, it achieves 80.3 MOTA, 77.3 IDF1, and 63.1 HOTA at 30 FPS, and it is integrated natively into the Ultralytics YOLOv8 API. For indoor wheelchair corridors, its recovery of low-confidence detections from partially occluded people is the algorithmically critical property. ByteTrack is the de facto default tracker for any YOLO-paired pipeline, and any alternative must demonstrate a clear advantage over it.

## Contradictions & Debates

**YOLOv8n versus YOLOv10n on Jetson Orin Nano.** This is a disagreement by absence rather than by conflicting measurements. Alqahtani et al. (2024) and Rey et al. (2025) provide validated latency data for YOLOv8n on the target hardware; Wang et al. (2024) establish YOLOv10's architectural superiority — 46% latency reduction over YOLOv9, NMS elimination reducing end-to-end overhead — exclusively on server-class GPUs. The architectural argument for YOLOv10n is strong, and the NeurIPS 2024 publication gives it institutional credibility. However, whether the NMS-free design exports cleanly to TensorRT's INT8 optimization graph, or introduces new friction in the compilation stage, is an empirical question that Wang et al. (2024) do not address. The team cannot resolve this from the published literature and must conduct the comparison on the target hardware. This constitutes a directly publishable benchmark contribution.

**ByteTrack versus OC-SORT for indoor corridor occlusion.** Zhang et al. (2022) and Cao et al. (2023) optimize for different failure modes, making their results complementary rather than directly comparable. ByteTrack's low-confidence association addresses the partial occlusion case — a person generating a weak detection due to a doorframe or partial obstruction — while OC-SORT's virtual trajectory addresses the extended occlusion case where a person disappears entirely behind furniture. Their headline figures (ByteTrack: 80.3 MOTA on MOT17; OC-SORT: state-of-the-art across MOT17, MOT20, KITTI, and DanceTrack) are not directly comparable because they optimize different metrics and use different evaluation protocol versions. In indoor wheelchair corridors, both failure modes occur simultaneously, and neither paper provides a head-to-head comparison on low-camera-height indoor scenarios. The pipeline proposal designates ByteTrack as the default and OC-SORT as the occlusion-heavy fallback, but this ordering reflects integration convenience and theoretical reasoning rather than an empirically established best practice.

**Embedded-optimized tracking (HopTrack) versus accuracy-optimized tracking (ByteTrack/OC-SORT).** HopTrack (Li et al., 2024) provides the only Jetson-specific tracker validation, at 39.29 FPS on AGX Xavier with 20.8% energy savings — properties that have genuine operational value for a battery-powered wheelchair. ByteTrack (Zhang et al., 2022) and OC-SORT (Cao et al., 2023) achieve superior MOTA figures but on server-class hardware without concurrent workloads. The embedded-constraint literature — Panopticus (Lee et al., 2024) and HopTrack (Li et al., 2024) — consistently demonstrates that accuracy advantages measured on server hardware do not translate intact to embedded deployment under concurrent multi-model workloads. Whether ByteTrack and OC-SORT maintain their accuracy advantage over HopTrack when running concurrently with YOLO, DR-SPAAM, and SLAM on the Orin Nano is not established by any shortlisted paper.

**Fusion sensitivity to sensor misalignment versus late fusion's calibration tolerance.** Tamim et al. (2026) find that their fusion model DAL "remains sensitive to sensor misalignment and LiDAR-based corruptions," a finding that motivates the pipeline's adoption of late fusion via spatial proximity matching rather than tight early fusion. However, Tamim et al. (2026) evaluate a specific early fusion architecture on JRDB under controlled conditions; they do not characterize how spatial proximity late fusion performs under real-world calibration drift on a physical wheelchair experiencing vibration and impacts. The comparison is therefore between an early fusion model with known misalignment sensitivity and a late fusion architecture with uncharacterized threshold sensitivity — neither paper fully resolves the calibration fragility question under realistic operational conditions.

**FP16 versus INT8 quantization for detection accuracy on safety-critical objects.** Alqahtani et al. (2024) benchmark INT8 and report 23 ms latency on the Orin Nano but do not report FP16 latency for comparison. Rey et al. (2025) compare INT8 against unquantized FP32 but do not report per-class accuracy degradation. No shortlisted paper characterizes how INT8 quantization specifically affects detection accuracy for the object classes most critical to wheelchair safety: partially occluded people, low-profile obstacles (bags, chair legs), and objects at the edge of the camera's vertical field of view. The safety asymmetry is large — a missed person detection carries far higher cost than a false positive — and the quantization accuracy literature does not address this regime.

## Open Questions & Research Gaps

- **What is the compound latency of the full pipeline stack running concurrently on Jetson Orin Nano?** Every shortlisted benchmark measures a single model or subsystem in isolation. The sum of isolated latency figures (23 ms YOLO + tracker overhead + ~44 ms DR-SPAAM equivalent + SLAM) does not account for GPU contention, memory bandwidth sharing, or interrupt scheduling under concurrent load. Alqahtani et al. (2024), Rey et al. (2025), and Pham et al. (2023) all measure single-task workloads. Characterizing end-to-end pipeline latency under realistic concurrent operation is the team's primary empirical gap and is directly publishable as a systems contribution.

- **How do YOLO detectors and MOT trackers perform at wheelchair camera height on JRDB?** Martín-Martín et al. (2021) provide the definitive egocentric indoor robot benchmark, but no shortlisted model paper evaluates YOLOv8n, YOLOv10n, ByteTrack, or OC-SORT on JRDB. The known viewpoint mismatch between MOT17/20 (standing height, outdoor/surveillance) and wheelchair deployment means published accuracy numbers are not directly transferable. Closing this gap — running the candidate detection and tracking components on JRDB at robot camera height — is achievable with the team's hardware and produces directly trustworthy accuracy estimates. This is a concrete, achievable experimental contribution.

- **Does DR-SPAAM meaningfully improve safety recall over RGB-D YOLO alone in wheelchair-height indoor scenarios?** The complementary coverage justification for the dual-sensor architecture is supported by Tamim et al.'s (2026) fusion superiority finding, but no paper measures the ablative recall improvement of camera+LiDAR over camera-only in a wheelchair-height indoor corridor scenario. Plozza et al. (2024) confirm the LiDAR layer works; Tamim et al. (2026) confirm fusion beats single modality; but the margin — how many additional safety-relevant detections LiDAR contributes beyond what YOLO already captures — has not been quantified in this operational context.

- **What is the effect of INT8 quantization on detection accuracy for safety-critical object classes?** The shortlist confirms INT8 reduces latency (Alqahtani et al., 2024; Rey et al., 2025) but no paper characterizes per-class accuracy degradation specifically for partially occluded people or low-profile obstacles. The wheelchair's safety requirements are asymmetric: a missed detection of a person is vastly more costly than a false positive. Whether INT8 quantization disproportionately degrades recall for the hardest detection cases — small, partially visible, or low-confidence objects — is uncharacterized. Alqahtani et al. (2024) explicitly flag robustness testing under occlusion as absent from their benchmark.

- **How should the late-fusion spatial proximity threshold be calibrated?** The pipeline proposal specifies 0.4 m as the RGB-D/LiDAR association threshold. No shortlisted paper characterizes the false positive and false negative rate of spatial proximity matching as a function of threshold value in a real indoor environment with varying sensor calibration quality. Tamim et al. (2026) raise sensor misalignment as a fusion failure mode but do not address threshold calibration. The sensitivity of the late-fusion architecture to this hyperparameter under realistic calibration drift is an open empirical question.

- **How do detection and tracking systems degrade under low-light and cluttered indoor conditions?** Alqahtani et al. (2024) explicitly flag the absence of robustness testing under occlusion and low-light as a limitation of their benchmark. Rey et al. (2025) do not test environmental robustness. Xu et al. (2023, 2024) test in crowded environments but not in degraded lighting. The shortlist has near-zero robustness evidence for degraded perception conditions — precisely the conditions that elevate wheelchair collision risk in real indoor environments.

- **What is the minimum viable scene representation for the LLM slow loop interface?** The pipeline's scene state message — structured JSON including per-object position, velocity, time-to-collision, and class label — is proposed without empirical validation that this representation is sufficient or optimal for LLM-based navigation intent inference. None of the shortlisted papers address the LLM interface design. This is joint work with the LLM teammates but is a concrete gap in the perception pipeline literature as it pertains to shared autonomy wheelchair systems.

- **How does tracking stability degrade as crowd density changes across the wheelchair's indoor environments?** ByteTrack (Zhang et al., 2022) and OC-SORT (Cao et al., 2023) are evaluated on crowd tracking benchmarks where aggregate MOTA across many tracks is the primary metric. The wheelchair's indoor corridor scenarios involve 1–5 people where individual track continuity matters far more than aggregate statistics. Performance at low pedestrian density — where losing a single track is a safety event — is not a primary focus of any shortlisted tracker paper.
