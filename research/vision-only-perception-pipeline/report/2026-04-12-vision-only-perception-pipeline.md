# Vision-Only Indoor Perception Pipeline for Shared-Autonomy Wheelchair: Depth Estimation, Visual SLAM, Tracking, and Edge Deployment

**Date:** 2026-04-12
**Topic:** vision-only-perception-pipeline
**Papers reviewed:** 17 shortlisted from 33 candidates
**Hardware target:** NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano (~20 TOPS INT8)

---

## Abstract

This literature review synthesizes 17 shortlisted papers addressing the design of a camera-only, real-time indoor perception pipeline for a shared-autonomy power wheelchair deployed on the NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano. The review spans five functional layers: monocular and stereo depth estimation, visual simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM), multi-object tracking and person re-identification, RGB-D semantic segmentation, and edge hardware benchmarking. Major findings indicate that real-time monocular depth inference is architecturally feasible on Jetson-class hardware (Feng et al., 2024), but a precision gap persists between neural depth estimates and hardware structured-light depth at the 0.5–4 m obstacle-avoidance range (Rustler et al., 2025). RTAB-Map with RGB-D is validated for shared-autonomy wheelchair SLAM (Xu et al., 2025; Labbe & Michaud, 2019), and ByteTrack is established as the default multi-object tracker for real-time YOLO-paired pipelines (Zhang et al., 2022). Three critical gaps remain unaddressed: the compound latency of the full concurrent stack on the Orin Nano, monocular depth accuracy at wheelchair camera height in indoor clinical environments, and visual SLAM robustness in featureless hospital corridors.

## Themes

### Theme 1: Monocular Depth Estimation Is Feasible on Edge Hardware but Faces a Precision Wall at Wheelchair-Relevant Ranges

The central question distinguishing a vision-only pipeline from a LiDAR-based baseline is whether monocular depth estimation can deliver sufficient metric accuracy at the 0.5–4 m range required for indoor obstacle avoidance. The shortlisted literature provides convergent but incomplete evidence that it can, subject to important caveats. Feng et al. (2024) deliver the most hardware-specific data: their RT-MonoDepth-S architecture achieves 30.5 FPS on the Jetson Nano and 364.1 FPS on the Jetson AGX Orin using 640×192 RGB images, demonstrating that lightweight encoder design, reduced feature resolution, and depthwise separable convolutions together enable real-time depth inference on constrained platforms. While the Orin Nano's ~20 TOPS compute lies between these two reference points and no published benchmark directly measures RT-MonoDepth-S on the Orin Nano, the architectural evidence from Feng et al. (2024) establishes that real-time monocular depth is a realistic design target rather than a theoretical possibility.

Yang et al. (2024, Depth Anything V2) and Yang et al. (2024, Depth Anything V1) establish the training paradigm that makes large-scale zero-shot monocular depth possible. Depth Anything V1 scales to 62 million unlabeled images with auxiliary semantic supervision (Yang et al., 2024, V1), while V2 replaces labeled real images with synthetic data and introduces pseudo-labeled real images for student model training, improving prediction fidelity and robustness across diverse scenes (Yang et al., 2024, V2). The V2 small variant at 25 million parameters is the relevant deployment choice for the Orin Nano, and metric depth variants of V2 provide absolute depth estimates essential for obstacle ranging without hardware sensors. The precision wall, however, emerges most concretely from Rustler et al. (2025): hardware structured-light depth on the Intel RealSense D435 achieves sub-1 cm error at close range, and the best stereo camera (ZED 2) maintains only 3 cm error at 4 m. Monocular neural depth cannot match these per-frame accuracy figures because monocular depth estimation is fundamentally an underdetermined problem resolved by learned priors (Yang et al., 2024, V1; Yang et al., 2024, V2). For a shared-autonomy wheelchair where a 20 cm localization error at 1 m carries genuine safety consequences, this precision gap is not trivial. Yu et al. (2024) extend the monocular approach to full 3D occupancy prediction from a single RGB image, demonstrating state-of-the-art results on the Occ-ScanNet benchmark — which is 40 times larger than NYUv2 and provides the most relevant indoor evaluation available — but provide no latency or memory characterization on edge hardware, leaving the feasibility of real-time 3D occupancy estimation on the Orin Nano unresolved. The literature therefore supports the pipeline's framing of hardware depth (Level A: RealSense D435i) as the preferred sensor configuration and monocular neural depth (Level C) as a cost-constrained fallback, a hierarchy justified by measured accuracy rather than convention.

### Theme 2: Stereo Camera Selection Is an Engineering Trade-off Across Sensor Capability, GPU Dependency, and Integrated AI

For pipeline Level B — stereo depth without monocular neural estimation — the choice among the Intel RealSense D435, StereoLabs ZED 2, and Luxonis OAK-D Pro determines the entire downstream compute architecture. Rustler et al. (2025) provide the only shortlisted paper offering a direct empirical comparison of all three cameras, evaluated across more than 3,000 frames per camera and three distinct scene scenarios. Their findings impose non-trivial design constraints: the RealSense D435 achieves sub-1 cm error at close range and is best suited to the 0–2 m working range of close-proximity robotics; the ZED 2 maintains depth accuracy within 3 cm at 4 m distances but requires external GPU processing for depth computation; and the OAK-D Pro offers integrated AI inference via its onboard Myriad X VPU, enabling lightweight neural models to run natively on the camera rather than on the host Jetson (Rustler et al., 2025). On the Jetson Orin Nano, the ZED 2's GPU dependency is not a marginal overhead — it directly competes with YOLOv8 object detection, depth estimation, and visual SLAM for the same shared GPU memory bus.

The learned stereo alternative — running deep stereo matching on the Orin Nano's GPU rather than relying on on-device camera processing — is represented by two shortlisted methods. Lipson et al. (2021) present RAFT-Stereo, which achieves top performance on the Middlebury indoor benchmark with 29% lower 1-pixel error than the next-best published method, using a multi-level convolutional GRU hierarchy whose depth is configurable for speed-accuracy trade-offs. Xu et al. (2023, IGEV-Stereo) introduce iterative geometry encoding volumes that provide a warm-start initialization for ConvGRU iterations, accelerating convergence; IGEV-Stereo ranks first on KITTI 2015 and 2012 and is the fastest method among the top-10 approaches (Xu et al., 2023). Both methods offer configurable latency and produce higher-quality disparity maps than traditional stereo algorithms. The architectural tension is that running either RAFT-Stereo or IGEV-Stereo on the Orin Nano's GPU alongside YOLOv8 detection and SLAM eliminates the compute advantage of using a stereo camera relative to the hardware-depth RealSense option. No shortlisted paper characterizes this GPU budget competition empirically for a concurrent full pipeline, making sensor selection an engineering trade-off that the existing literature informs but does not resolve.

### Theme 3: Visual SLAM for Indoor Wheelchair Navigation Requires Confronting Featureless Environments — the Literature Provides Algorithms but Not the Robustness Data

The SLAM layer replaces 2D LiDAR localization and mapping in the vision-only pipeline, and the shortlisted SLAM systems — Campos et al. (2021, ORB-SLAM3) and Labbe & Michaud (2019, RTAB-Map) — are mature, extensively validated libraries. Yet neither was evaluated under the specific conditions that make wheelchair deployment challenging: long featureless corridors, low-texture painted walls, fluorescent overhead lighting, and a camera mounted at 0.8–1.2 m height rather than the 1.5–1.8 m height standard in most SLAM evaluations. Campos et al. (2021) demonstrate ORB-SLAM3's accuracy comprehensively: stereo-inertial SLAM achieves 3.6 cm average accuracy on EuRoC drone corridor sequences and 9 mm on TUM-VI indoor room sequences, with the tracking thread operating at 30–40 FPS on standard workstation hardware. The library's unified support for monocular, stereo, and RGB-D cameras within a single codebase is architecturally attractive for a pipeline that operates across three sensor hardware levels. However, ORB-SLAM3 requires sufficient visual features — corners, edges, textures — to maintain localization, a requirement the pipeline proposal explicitly identifies as the primary operational risk for hospital corridors with blank painted walls (Campos et al., 2021). Labbe & Michaud (2019) make RTAB-Map the preferred alternative when the output must be a 2D occupancy grid compatible with Nav2's costmap format; RTAB-Map's memory management approach, which limits the set of locations used for loop closure detection to maintain real-time constraints as the map grows, is directly relevant to multi-room wheelchair navigation where a naive graph-SLAM approach would accumulate unbounded memory (Labbe & Michaud, 2019).

The strongest available evidence for RTAB-Map in the wheelchair context comes from Xu et al. (2025, CoNav Chair), whose ROS-based shared-autonomy wheelchair uses RTAB-Map with an RGB-D sensor as the primary mapping input, demonstrating real indoor corridor navigation with a statistically significant reduction in collisions compared to full-manual and full-autonomous modes. Rosinol et al. (2020, Kimera) add a third SLAM option with a distinct architectural advantage: CPU-only real-time operation producing a semantically labeled 3D mesh, which leaves the Orin Nano's GPU entirely available for YOLOv8 and depth estimation without resource competition (Rosinol et al., 2020). Ou et al. (2022) address the dynamic object problem all three SLAM systems face: their assistive navigation system for visually impaired people uses panoptic segmentation to identify and remove moving pedestrians from the SLAM feature set in real indoor environments, preventing map corruption — validating the dynamic object removal step the pipeline proposal specifies as a required integration with ORB-SLAM3 and RTAB-Map (Ou et al., 2022). Across all three SLAM systems, the open question is robustness under the specific failure modes of wheelchair deployment: sudden stops, rapid turns, dense dynamic obstacle presence, and the low camera height that reduces visible feature density relative to standing-height SLAM evaluations.

### Theme 4: Multi-Object Tracking and Person Re-Identification Are Functionally Separable but Operationally Coupled in Indoor Corridor Navigation

The tracking layer must address two interacting problems: maintaining trajectory continuity for partially visible or momentarily occluded objects, and recovering identity for objects that leave the camera frame entirely and return from a different direction. Zhang et al. (2022, ByteTrack) solve the occlusion-recovery problem through low-confidence detection association: rather than discarding detections below the confidence threshold, ByteTrack associates weak detections against existing tracklets via IoU similarity, recovering objects partially behind door frames or furniture. This strategy achieves 80.3 MOTA, 77.3 IDF1, and 63.1 HOTA on MOT17 at 30 FPS on a V100, with consistent 1–10 IDF1 point improvements across nine state-of-the-art detectors (Zhang et al., 2022). ByteTrack's native integration into Ultralytics' `model.track()` API makes it the lowest-friction default for a YOLO-paired pipeline. The critical limitation for indoor corridors is that ByteTrack's re-identification after complete disappearance relies on Kalman filter positional prediction — a mechanism that degrades when a person walks through a doorway and re-enters from an unexpected direction (Zhang et al., 2022).

The person re-identification problem — recovering the same individual's identity after they have been entirely outside the camera field of view — is addressed by Syed et al. (2023) with a lightweight CNN incorporating part-wise channel and spatial attention, achieving 80.73% Rank-1 accuracy on PRID and 64.44% on VIPeR at 29.4 ms inference on an embedded Hi3516DV300 camera chip via 88% computation reduction through depthwise separable convolutions. The part-wise attention mechanism, which divides the body into horizontal bands and learns inter-band relationships, explicitly handles the viewpoint and illumination variation dominant in indoor corridor re-ID (Syed et al., 2023). A complementary motion detection layer is provided by Teed & Deng (2020, RAFT): optical flow computed independently of object classification can flag any moving pixel region, catching dynamic obstacles — fast-moving carts, low-profile objects — that YOLO may miss between detection frames. The RAFT-S smaller variant at 2.7 million parameters is deployable at real-time FPS on Jetson-class hardware and is designated in the pipeline proposal to run at 5–10 FPS as an independent motion mask alongside YOLO detections and ByteTrack tracks (Teed & Deng, 2020). The operational coupling between tracking and re-ID becomes critical at longer approach distances — when a person first appears at 4 m, YOLO may detect with low confidence, ByteTrack must maintain the track through the approach, and re-ID must recover the track after brief occlusion — but this end-to-end scenario is not characterized in any single shortlisted paper.

### Theme 5: RGB-D Semantic Segmentation Provides Richer Scene Representations Than Geometric SLAM, but GPU Cost Creates an Architectural Trade-off

The SLAM layer's geometric output — a sparse point cloud from ORB-SLAM3 or a binary occupancy grid from RTAB-Map — does not distinguish between a door, a wall, a person, and a piece of furniture. Semantic understanding is directly required by the shared-autonomy pipeline's LLM slow loop, which must interpret structured scene descriptions to produce navigation intent. Seichter et al. (2021, ESANet) demonstrate efficient RGB-D semantic segmentation optimized for NVIDIA TensorRT on a Jetson AGX Xavier — the same Ampere architecture family as the Orin Nano — evaluated on NYUv2 and SUNRGB-D indoor benchmarks. The practical robot deployment described by Seichter et al. (2021) demonstrates free-space identification, person detection, and navigation on Jetson AGX Xavier, providing a directly transferable TensorRT optimization path to the Orin Nano.

Yu et al. (2024, ISO) extend the representational ambition from 2D per-pixel labels to full 3D occupancy prediction from a single monocular RGB image, leveraging a pretrained depth predictor backbone and the D-FLoSP module for voxel feature learning, and achieving state-of-the-art results on both NYUv2 and Occ-ScanNet. The appeal is direct: a 3D occupancy map from the same camera used for YOLO detection would enable obstacle avoidance without any hardware depth sensor. However, ISO's inference latency and GPU memory requirements on edge hardware are entirely uncharacterized by Yu et al. (2024), leaving the approach's real-time feasibility on the Orin Nano open. Rosinol et al. (2020, Kimera) offer a semantically enriched SLAM alternative: by piping semantically labeled images into a 3D mesh reconstruction module, Kimera produces a metric-semantic mesh with room and furniture labels during CPU-only real-time operation, leaving the Orin Nano's GPU entirely available for detection and depth (Rosinol et al., 2020). The three approaches — ESANet's 2D GPU segmentation, ISO's 3D monocular occupancy, and Kimera's CPU semantic mesh — produce distinct output representations that must each be converted to an occupancy grid for Nav2 integration, and no shortlisted paper compares them head-to-head on the Orin Nano for wheelchair-relevant tasks.

### Theme 6: The Gap Between Academic Benchmarks and Wheelchair-Specific Deployment Conditions Is Larger for Vision-Only Than for LiDAR Pipelines

The deployment gap affecting the vision-only pipeline manifests in three simultaneous ways: viewpoint height mismatch, metric depth calibration to the target domain, and the indoor/outdoor training domain shift in depth estimation. Depth estimation models in the shortlist — Yang et al. (2024, V1 and V2) and Feng et al. (2024) — were trained and evaluated primarily on outdoor driving data (KITTI) with supplementary indoor evaluation on NYUv2. A wheelchair camera at 0.8–1.2 m height sees dramatically more floor, less upper wall, and foreshortened furniture at angles underrepresented in all standard indoor depth benchmarks. Yang et al. (2024, V2) partially address domain generalization by training on synthetic data spanning diverse indoor scenes, but no shortlisted paper evaluates monocular depth at wheelchair camera height in hospital or care facility corridors.

The stereo and SLAM papers share this viewpoint gap. RAFT-Stereo is evaluated on Middlebury and ETH3D at conventional camera heights (Lipson et al., 2021); IGEV-Stereo focuses on KITTI outdoor evaluation (Xu et al., 2023); ORB-SLAM3 reports EuRoC drone-corridor results and TUM-VI handheld room sequences (Campos et al., 2021). RTAB-Map's evaluation on TUM RGB-D (Labbe & Michaud, 2019) is the closest benchmark match to the wheelchair context since the TUM dataset uses handheld RGB-D cameras in indoor rooms, approximating the wheelchair's environment if not its camera height. Alqahtani et al. (2024) provide the only shortlisted measurement directly on the Jetson Orin Nano — benchmarking YOLOv8 variants for detection-only workloads — and confirm the Orin Nano is the fastest tested edge device, but this single-stage benchmark does not address compound pipeline latency. Ou et al. (2022) come closest to the target deployment domain, using an RGB-D sensor in indoor corridors at a body height approximating wheelchair camera geometry and demonstrating dynamic obstacle velocity tracking in real environments. Xu et al. (2025, CoNav Chair) represent the only shortlisted paper operating an actual shared-autonomy wheelchair with visual SLAM and RGB-D in real indoor environments, but as a preprint with limited quantitative methodology it cannot substitute for peer-reviewed benchmark data. The vision-only pipeline carries greater empirical uncertainty than the LiDAR baseline precisely because LiDAR depth is governed by sensor physics and transfers well across viewpoints, while monocular depth estimation's learned priors are highly sensitive to training domain characteristics that no shortlisted paper has matched to the wheelchair deployment context.

## Key Papers

**Alqahtani et al. (2024)** — [Benchmarking Deep Learning Models for Object Detection on Edge Computing Devices](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.16808)
*arXiv preprint, 2024*
Alqahtani et al. (2024) evaluate YOLOv8 variants, EfficientDet Lite, and SSD on resource-constrained edge devices including the Raspberry Pi and Jetson Orin Nano, measuring energy consumption, inference speed, and detection accuracy across configurations. The central finding — that the Jetson Orin Nano delivers the fastest processing among tested devices despite the highest idle power consumption — directly informs duty-cycle and power-budget decisions for the wheelchair pipeline. This is the only shortlisted paper providing empirical FPS and energy data for YOLOv8 (the pipeline's detection backbone) on the exact target hardware, making it the primary performance anchor for the fast safety loop's 50 ms latency budget.

**Campos et al. (2021)** — [ORB-SLAM3: An Accurate Open-Source Library for Visual, Visual-Inertial and Multi-Map SLAM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.11898)
*IEEE Transactions on Robotics, 2021*
Campos et al. (2021) present the first library performing visual, visual-inertial, and multi-map SLAM across monocular, stereo, and RGB-D cameras using tightly coupled maximum-a-posteriori visual-inertial estimation and place-recognition-triggered multi-map merging. Stereo-inertial SLAM achieves 3.6 cm average accuracy on EuRoC and 9 mm on TUM-VI indoor room sequences, with the tracking thread operating at 30–40 FPS on standard hardware — establishing the accuracy ceiling for visual SLAM available to the wheelchair pipeline. The library's unified multi-modality support across all three pipeline sensor hardware levels (monocular, stereo, RGB-D) is its primary architectural advantage, and its peer-reviewed IEEE TRO publication with comprehensive multi-dataset evaluation makes it the foundational SLAM reference.

**Feng et al. (2024)** — [Real-time Monocular Depth Estimation on Embedded Systems](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.10569)
*ICIP 2024*
Feng et al. (2024) propose RT-MonoDepth and RT-MonoDepth-S, lightweight neural architectures achieving competitive KITTI depth accuracy while enabling real-time inference on Jetson-class hardware: RT-MonoDepth-S reaches 30.5 FPS on Jetson Nano and 364.1 FPS on Jetson AGX Orin at 640×192 resolution. The architectural design principles — lightweight encoder, reduced feature resolution, depthwise separable convolutions — establish a concrete template for monocular depth deployment on the Orin Nano. This paper is the primary hardware-specific benchmark for monocular depth estimation in the shortlist, though its KITTI-only evaluation does not characterize indoor obstacle accuracy at wheelchair-relevant ranges.

**Labbe & Michaud (2019)** — [RTAB-Map as an Open-Source Lidar and Visual SLAM Library for Large-Scale and Long-Term Online Operation](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/rob.21831)
*Journal of Field Robotics, 2019*
Labbe & Michaud (2019) describe RTAB-Map's graph-based SLAM with an incremental bag-of-words loop closure detector and a memory management mechanism that bounds the working set used for loop closure detection, enabling real-time operation during long-term deployments without unbounded memory growth. Multi-dataset validation spanning KITTI, EuRoC, TUM RGB-D, and MIT Stata Center demonstrates generality across sensor types, with TUM RGB-D providing the most directly relevant indoor evaluation. The native 2D occupancy grid output compatible with Nav2's costmap format and active ROS integration make RTAB-Map the recommended production SLAM library for the wheelchair pipeline, corroborated by its deployment in CoNav Chair (Xu et al., 2025).

**Lipson et al. (2021)** — [RAFT-Stereo: Multilevel Recurrent Field Transforms for Stereo Matching](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.07547)
*3DV 2021*
Lipson et al. (2021) build RAFT-Stereo on the RAFT optical flow framework, introducing multi-level convolutional GRUs that propagate information across image scales to produce high-quality disparity maps, achieving top performance on Middlebury with 29% lower 1-pixel error than the next-best published method and strong results on ETH3D indoor scenes. A real-time variant configures GRU hierarchy depth for faster inference, making RAFT-Stereo a viable learned stereo option on Jetson-class hardware when the pipeline uses an OAK-D or ZED stereo camera. The memory-efficient design supporting full-resolution stereo on megapixel images is relevant for future pipeline upgrades beyond the current 640×480 resolution.

**Ou et al. (2022)** — [Indoor Navigation Assistance for Visually Impaired People via Dynamic SLAM and Panoptic Segmentation with an RGB-D Sensor](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.01154)
*ICCHP 2022*
Ou et al. (2022) combine panoptic segmentation, ego-motion estimation, and dynamic obstacle velocity tracking in an assistive indoor navigation system using an RGB-D sensor, demonstrating that moving pedestrians can be identified and removed from SLAM feature sets to prevent map corruption in real indoor corridor environments. The system determines dynamic obstacle positions and velocities and communicates hazards acoustically, mapping almost exactly to the wheelchair pipeline's requirements for dynamic object removal from the SLAM map and velocity-tagged obstacle output in the structured scene state. This paper provides the primary validation for the dynamic object removal design step and represents the closest analogous system to the wheelchair pipeline in the assistive technology literature.

**Rosinol et al. (2020)** — [Kimera: An Open-Source Library for Real-Time Metric-Semantic Localization and Mapping](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.02490)
*ICRA 2020*
Rosinol et al. (2020) present Kimera, a modular C++ library combining visual-inertial odometry, pose graph optimization, 3D mesh reconstruction, and semantic labeling from a camera-IMU pair, with the key property that all components run in real time on CPU without requiring GPU acceleration. The semantic mesh output — labeled with floor, wall, furniture, and other indoor categories — enables the scene understanding that the LLM slow loop requires beyond raw occupancy grids. CPU-only operation is architecturally significant for the Orin Nano: Kimera can run concurrently with GPU-bound YOLOv8 detection and Depth Anything V2 estimation without direct resource competition, making it the semantically richest SLAM alternative with the lowest GPU cost.

**Rustler et al. (2025)** — [Empirical Comparison of Four Stereoscopic Depth Sensing Cameras for Robotics Applications](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.07421)
*IEEE Access, 2025*
Rustler et al. (2025) evaluate the Intel RealSense D435, D455, StereoLabs ZED 2, and Luxonis OAK-D Pro across three distinct scene scenarios using more than 3,000 frames per camera, finding sub-1 cm close-range error for the D435, sub-3 cm at 4 m for the ZED 2, and identifying the ZED 2's external GPU dependency and OAK-D Pro's integrated AI inference capability as fundamental architectural constraints. These findings directly inform sensor selection for the wheelchair pipeline: the RealSense D435 emerges as the most appropriate Level A sensor for 0–4 m indoor ranging with minimal compute overhead, while the ZED 2's GPU dependency places it in direct competition with pipeline components on the Orin Nano. This is the only shortlisted paper providing direct empirical comparison of the three camera families under active consideration for this project, and its publicly available 12,000+ frame dataset enables independent validation.

**Seichter et al. (2021)** — [Efficient RGB-D Semantic Segmentation for Indoor Scene Analysis](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06961)
*ICRA 2021*
Seichter et al. (2021) present ESANet, an RGB-D segmentation architecture optimized for NVIDIA TensorRT on a Jetson AGX Xavier, evaluated on NYUv2 and SUNRGB-D indoor benchmarks and validated in a practical indoor robot deployment demonstrating free-space identification, person detection, and navigation. Competitive accuracy with faster inference than contemporary methods, combined with evaluation on the same Ampere architecture family as the Orin Nano, provides a directly transferable path to on-device semantic segmentation at the frequencies needed for the pipeline's LLM slow loop. ESANet is the strongest validated candidate for the semantic scene understanding layer among the shortlisted papers, with indoor benchmark evaluation and explicit robot navigation demonstration.

**Syed et al. (2023)** — [Lightweight Multimodal Domain Generic Person Reidentification Metric for Person-Following Robots](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9866428/)
*Sensors (Basel), 2023*
Syed et al. (2023) develop a person re-identification metric combining a lightweight CNN backbone with part-wise channel and spatial attention, achieving 80.73% Rank-1 accuracy on PRID and 64.44% on VIPeR at 29.4 ms inference on an embedded Hi3516DV300 camera chip via 88% computation reduction through depthwise separable convolutions. The part-wise attention mechanism, dividing the body into horizontal bands and learning inter-band relationships, explicitly handles the viewpoint and illumination variation dominant in indoor corridor re-ID scenarios. This is the only shortlisted re-ID paper with explicit embedded hardware deployment validation, making it the primary person re-identification reference for the pipeline's tracker layer.

**Teed & Deng (2020)** — [RAFT: Recurrent All-Pairs Field Transforms for Optical Flow](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.12039)
*ECCV 2020 (Best Paper)*
Teed & Deng (2020) propose RAFT, which extracts per-pixel features, constructs multi-scale all-pairs correlation volumes, and iteratively refines flow estimates using a lightweight 2.7M-parameter recurrent update operator, achieving 5.10% F1-all on KITTI and 2.855 end-point-error on Sintel — 16% and 30% improvements over prior state of the art respectively. The RAFT-S smaller variant is deployable at real-time FPS on Jetson-class hardware and is designated in the pipeline proposal to run at 5–10 FPS as an independent motion mask alongside YOLO detections and ByteTrack tracks. RAFT's role in the pipeline is to detect any moving pixel region — fast-moving carts, low-profile objects — regardless of whether YOLO assigns a confident class label, providing a complementary motion detection signal not available from classification-based tracking alone.

**Xu et al. (2023, IGEV-Stereo)** — [Iterative Geometry Encoding Volume for Stereo Matching](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.06615)
*CVPR 2023*
Xu et al. (2023) construct a geometry encoding volume combining geometry, context, and local matching information, using iterative ConvGRU indexing with a warm-start initialization that regresses an accurate disparity starting point and thereby reduces the number of ConvGRU iterations required for convergence. IGEV-Stereo ranks first on KITTI 2015 and 2012 benchmarks and is the fastest method among the top-10 published approaches, with strong cross-dataset generalization relevant to deployment in novel indoor environments. The accelerated convergence and configurable iteration count make IGEV-Stereo the preferred learned stereo method when strict latency constraints apply alongside a stereo camera on the Orin Nano.

**Xu et al. (2025, CoNav Chair)** — [CoNav Chair: Design of a ROS-based Smart Wheelchair for Shared Control Navigation in the Built Environment](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.09680)
*arXiv preprint, 2025*
Xu et al. (2025) present a ROS-based shared-autonomy wheelchair integrating LiDAR, RGB-D camera, IMU, and encoders, using RTAB-Map for 3D mapping with the RGB-D sensor as primary input and a shared control framework that balances autonomous obstacle avoidance with user-directed navigation. Real indoor corridor and room evaluations demonstrate significantly fewer collisions and comparable task completion time versus full manual or full autonomous modes, validating both the shared-autonomy paradigm and RTAB-Map's specific applicability to wheelchair navigation. This is the closest available system-level reference to the proposed pipeline, providing direct architectural precedent for the RTAB-Map and RGB-D camera combination.

**Yang et al. (2024, Depth Anything V2)** — [Depth Anything V2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.09414)
*NeurIPS 2024*
Yang et al. (2024, V2) improve on Depth Anything V1 through three strategies — replacing labeled real training images with synthetic data, scaling up teacher model capacity, and training student models on large-scale pseudo-labeled real images — producing a model family from 25M to 1.3B parameters with finer, more robust depth predictions and metric depth variants. The small variant at 25M parameters is feasible for Jetson Orin Nano deployment, with metric depth variants providing absolute depth estimates essential for obstacle ranging without hardware sensors. Zero-shot generalization across diverse indoor and outdoor scenes, combined with synthetic indoor training data, makes Depth Anything V2 the recommended neural depth backbone for pipeline Level C and as a complementary layer under Level A.

**Yu et al. (2024, ISO)** — [Monocular Occupancy Prediction for Scalable Indoor Scenes](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.11730)
*ECCV 2024*
Yu et al. (2024) present ISO, which estimates full 3D indoor occupancy from single RGB images using a pretrained depth predictor backbone and the D-FLoSP module for dual feature line-of-sight voxel projection, achieving state-of-the-art performance on both NYUv2 and the newly released Occ-ScanNet benchmark (40 times the size of NYUv2). The appeal for the wheelchair pipeline is direct: full 3D occupancy from the detection camera alone would enable obstacle avoidance without any hardware depth sensor, using an output representation richer than 2D bounding boxes. Edge deployment latency is entirely uncharacterized by Yu et al. (2024), making ISO's feasibility on the Orin Nano an open experimental question that must be resolved before committing this module to any real-time pipeline stage.

**Zhang et al. (2022, ByteTrack)** — [ByteTrack: Multi-Object Tracking by Associating Every Detection Box](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.06864)
*ECCV 2022*
Zhang et al. (2022) demonstrate that associating nearly every detection box — including low-confidence ones below the standard threshold — against existing tracklets via IoU similarity recovers objects during partial occlusion and reduces track fragmentation, achieving 80.3 MOTA, 77.3 IDF1, and 63.1 HOTA on MOT17 at 30 FPS on a V100 with consistent 1–10 IDF1 point improvements across nine detectors. Low-confidence detection association is directly valuable for wheelchair corridor tracking where pedestrians partially occluded by doorframes or furniture must remain tracked through brief occlusion without dropping track ID. ByteTrack's native integration into Ultralytics' `model.track()` API eliminates implementation overhead, establishing it as the lowest-friction default tracker for the YOLO-paired pipeline.

## Contradictions & Debates

**Monocular neural depth versus hardware structured-light depth.** Feng et al. (2024) and Yang et al. (2024, V2) together demonstrate that real-time monocular depth inference on Jetson-class hardware is architecturally achievable and that metric depth variants can produce absolute distance estimates without hardware sensors. Against this, Rustler et al. (2025) establish empirically that hardware structured-light depth (RealSense D435, sub-1 cm at close range) and stereo hardware depth (ZED 2, 3 cm at 4 m) outperform reported monocular depth accuracy on a per-frame basis. The literature does not resolve whether monocular metric depth from Depth Anything V2-small meets the accuracy threshold required for safe wheelchair obstacle avoidance at 0.5–4 m — because that threshold is not defined anywhere in the shortlisted papers. The disagreement is therefore not about which depth method is more accurate, but about whether monocular depth's accuracy level is sufficient, and the existing evidence provides no answer for the wheelchair-specific operating range.

**ORB-SLAM3 versus RTAB-Map as the primary SLAM library.** Campos et al. (2021) establish ORB-SLAM3's superior localization accuracy (3.6 cm on EuRoC, 9 mm on TUM-VI) and its unified multi-modality support, making it the technically stronger SLAM system on published benchmarks. Labbe & Michaud (2019) and Xu et al. (2025, CoNav Chair) establish RTAB-Map's direct Nav2 costmap compatibility, memory-managed long-term operation, and deployed use in an actual shared-autonomy wheelchair. These camps do not contradict each other on technical facts but lead to incompatible architectural choices. The strongest resolution point is Xu et al. (2025), providing practical deployment evidence for RTAB-Map in exactly the target context — but as a preprint, its authority is limited, and the comparison was never made on the same hardware or in the same environment as Campos et al. (2021).

**Learned stereo matching (RAFT-Stereo, IGEV-Stereo) versus on-device camera stereo processing (OAK-D VPU, ZED on-camera).** Lipson et al. (2021) demonstrate that RAFT-Stereo achieves 29% lower Middlebury 1-pixel error than prior best methods; Xu et al. (2023) show IGEV-Stereo is the fastest top-10 KITTI method. These accuracy advantages are measured on server GPUs. Rustler et al. (2025) show the ZED 2 achieves competitive real-world indoor accuracy with its on-device processing, and the OAK-D Pro's onboard VPU offloads computation from the host entirely. The contradiction is that running learned stereo on the Orin Nano's GPU places it in direct competition with YOLOv8 detection, Depth Anything V2, and RAFT optical flow for the same compute budget, whereas on-device stereo offloads this at the cost of disparity quality. No shortlisted paper measures this GPU contention empirically for any concurrent pipeline configuration, making the comparison speculative at the component-in-isolation benchmark level.

**ISO's 3D monocular occupancy versus ESANet's 2D RGB-D segmentation for semantic scene understanding.** Yu et al. (2024) provide volumetric 3D obstacle representations from a single camera — informationally richer than 2D segmentation labels and requiring no hardware depth sensor. Seichter et al. (2021) provide validated indoor segmentation at real-time FPS on Jetson AGX Xavier hardware with an explicit robot navigation demonstration, producing 2D per-pixel semantic labels rather than 3D structure. The disagreement is not methodological: ISO is richer but its edge deployment latency is entirely uncharacterized (Yu et al., 2024), while ESANet's is validated on target-architecture hardware (Seichter et al., 2021). If ISO runs at 5+ FPS on the Orin Nano, it dominates ESANet for the asynchronous map-building role; if it requires multiple seconds per frame, it cannot contribute to any pipeline stage. This empirical question cannot be resolved from the published literature.

**Kimera's CPU-only semantic SLAM versus ESANet's GPU-optimized 2D segmentation.** Rosinol et al. (2020) claim real-time CPU operation for Kimera's metric-semantic 3D mesh construction, which would leave the Orin Nano's GPU entirely free for detection and depth estimation. Seichter et al. (2021) require TensorRT GPU inference but deliver per-frame 2D semantic labels rather than a gradually accumulated mesh. These approaches produce different output formats (3D mesh versus 2D label map) that are not directly comparable, and neither produces a Nav2-ready occupancy grid natively. The Nav2 costmap interface requires an occupancy grid, and the conversion step from either representation is uncharacterized by either paper. The choice between them depends on what the LLM slow loop and Nav2 costmap actually require at runtime — a question neither paper addresses.

**ByteTrack's low-confidence association behavior in sparse indoor scenes.** Zhang et al. (2022) calibrate ByteTrack's low-confidence detection association on MOT17 and MOT20, high-density pedestrian benchmarks with 10–80 simultaneous tracks, where the false positive cost from aggressive association is relatively low. In the wheelchair's indoor environment with 1–5 moving obstacles, false positive track associations from low-confidence background regions carry a different risk profile: a phantom obstacle detection at corridor width could cause the wheelchair to halt unnecessarily, while a missed-person false negative poses a collision risk. The safety implications of these two error types are asymmetric in the wheelchair context and are not characterized for sparse-scene operation in any shortlisted paper. The appropriate confidence thresholds for ByteTrack in the wheelchair setting may differ substantially from the MOT17-optimized defaults, and this calibration question remains open.

## Open Questions & Research Gaps

- **What is the metric depth accuracy of Depth Anything V2-small on the Jetson Orin Nano at wheelchair camera height (0.8–1.2 m) for indoor obstacles at 0.5–4 m range?** This is the pipeline's most critical empirical gap. All depth accuracy evidence in the shortlist derives from KITTI outdoor driving or standard indoor benchmarks at standing height (Yang et al., 2024, V1; Yang et al., 2024, V2; Feng et al., 2024). No shortlisted paper evaluates metric monocular depth at wheelchair camera geometry in hospital or care facility corridors. This experiment is directly publishable and determines whether Level C pipeline configuration is safe to deploy.

- **What is the compound latency of the full concurrent vision-only stack — YOLO + ByteTrack + depth estimation + ORB-SLAM3 or RTAB-Map + optical flow — running simultaneously on the Jetson Orin Nano?** Every shortlisted benchmark measures components in isolation on heterogeneous hardware: Alqahtani et al. (2024) measure YOLOv8 alone on the Orin Nano; Feng et al. (2024) measure RT-MonoDepth-S alone on the Jetson Nano and AGX Orin; Campos et al. (2021) measure ORB-SLAM3 tracking on a workstation. GPU memory contention when detection, depth estimation, and optical flow compete for the Orin Nano's shared memory bus is not characterized in any shortlisted paper, making the 50 ms fast-loop latency target an extrapolation from isolated benchmarks rather than a measured fact.

- **Does ORB-SLAM3 maintain stable localization in long featureless indoor corridors typical of hospitals and care facilities?** Campos et al. (2021) evaluate on EuRoC (drone corridors with rich texture) and TUM-VI (indoor rooms with furniture and varied surfaces). Neither dataset contains the uniform blank-painted walls characteristic of medical facilities. The pipeline proposal flags this as the primary operational risk and proposes ArUco fiducial markers as mitigation, but no shortlisted paper quantifies ORB-SLAM3's localization degradation rate in low-feature environments or the marker density required to maintain stability, leaving the mitigation strategy empirically unvalidated.

- **Is ISO's 3D occupancy prediction feasible at any real-time or near-real-time FPS on the Jetson Orin Nano?** Yu et al. (2024) report state-of-the-art accuracy on NYUv2 and Occ-ScanNet but provide no latency, throughput, or GPU memory characterization on any edge hardware. The D-FLoSP module's 3D voxel feature learning likely carries substantially higher compute cost than 2D segmentation. Whether ISO can run at even 5 FPS — the minimum suitable for asynchronous background map building — on the Orin Nano is entirely unknown from the published literature.

- **How does Depth Anything V2-small handle floor-level obstacles — bags, power cables, wheelchair footrests — at low camera height?** Floor-level obstacles are underrepresented in every training dataset used by the shortlisted depth models: KITTI outdoor driving, NYUv2 room scanning, and ScanNet (Yang et al., 2024, V1; Yang et al., 2024, V2; Feng et al., 2024). These objects are precisely the highest-risk detection failure mode for wheelchair navigation. No shortlisted paper quantifies how monocular depth accuracy degrades for objects at floor-plane level when captured from a 0.8–1.2 m camera height.

- **Can ByteTrack's default confidence thresholds be safely applied in the wheelchair's sparse indoor obstacle scene without generating phantom obstacle tracks?** Zhang et al. (2022) calibrate ByteTrack for high-density pedestrian benchmarks with 10–80 simultaneous tracks. In the wheelchair's 1–5 obstacle indoor environment with asymmetric safety consequences for missed versus phantom detections, the appropriate confidence threshold regime is not characterized by any shortlisted paper, nor is there evidence from sparse-scene ByteTrack deployments.

- **What is the minimum viable metric depth accuracy for wheelchair obstacle avoidance, and does the acceptable error threshold vary by obstacle type or approach distance?** No shortlisted paper — and no paper in the candidate set — defines a safety threshold for depth accuracy in wheelchair obstacle avoidance. Without this criterion, the success condition for the depth estimation module cannot be established from the literature, and experimental validation must define the threshold empirically before it can be applied to evaluate monocular versus hardware depth.

- **How does RTAB-Map's memory management perform during clinical-day-length deployments (6–8 hours) with recurring room visits?** Labbe & Michaud (2019) validate long-term operation on the MIT Stata Center, which is encouraging for multi-room navigation. The wheelchair-specific question — whether memory bounds prevent map degradation when the same rooms are visited repeatedly over a full working shift — is not addressed by any shortlisted paper, and map degradation during long-term operation could cause progressive localization error and compromise navigation safety.

- **What scene representation format best serves the LLM slow loop's navigation reasoning?** The pipeline proposal defines a structured JSON scene state message, but no shortlisted paper addresses how dynamic scene state should be represented for language model consumption. Neither the tracking papers (Zhang et al., 2022; Syed et al., 2023) nor the SLAM papers (Campos et al., 2021; Labbe & Michaud, 2019) provide guidance on this interface, and the format choice is not empirically validated against any LLM reasoning requirement. This is a joint open question with the LLM teammate subagents.

- **How does detection recall degrade in low-light indoor conditions?** All depth estimation and detection models in the shortlist assume adequate illumination (Yang et al., 2024, V1; Yang et al., 2024, V2; Feng et al., 2024; Alqahtani et al., 2024). RGB-based YOLO detection degrades in low-light environments — patient rooms with dimmed lighting, evening corridors — which are common in clinical deployment scenarios. Unlike LiDAR, which operates in darkness, camera pipelines have a hard illumination dependency that no shortlisted paper has characterized for indoor wheelchair-relevant scenarios or quantified against a safety threshold.
