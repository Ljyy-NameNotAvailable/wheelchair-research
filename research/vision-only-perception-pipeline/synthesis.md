# Synthesis: Vision-Only Real-Time Indoor Perception Pipeline for a Shared-Autonomy Power Wheelchair on Jetson Orin Nano

**Topic:** Camera-only (no LiDAR) real-time indoor perception feeding a shared-autonomy wheelchair on NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano (~20 TOPS INT8)
**Shortlisted papers:** 17 shortlisted (16 excluded with rationale)
**Synthesis date:** 2026-04-10
**Contrast reference:** Prior LiDAR pipeline synthesis (indoor-wheelchair-perception-pipeline/synthesis.md)

---

## Major Themes

### Theme 1: Monocular Depth Is Now Feasible on Edge Hardware but Faces a Precision Wall at the Ranges That Matter for Wheelchair Safety

The single biggest question distinguishing the vision-only pipeline from the LiDAR baseline is whether monocular depth estimation can deliver sufficient metric accuracy at the 0.5–4 m obstacle-avoidance range on the Jetson Orin Nano. The shortlist provides convergent but incomplete evidence that it can, subject to important caveats.

Feng et al. (2024) provide the most hardware-specific answer: RT-MonoDepth-S achieves 30.5 FPS on a Jetson Nano (the predecessor chip) and 364.1 FPS on a Jetson AGX Orin using 640×192 RGB images. The AGX Orin number is encouraging but not directly transferable — the AGX Orin has 32–275 TOPS depending on configuration, far above the Orin Nano's ~20 TOPS. The practical implication for the Orin Nano is that RT-MonoDepth-S likely lands somewhere between these extremes, probably in the 40–80 FPS range, but no published benchmark confirms this. What Feng et al. (2024) demonstrate clearly is that the architectural choices enabling edge deployment (lightweight encoder, reduced feature resolution, depthwise separable convolutions) are compatible with real-time depth — the question of absolute accuracy on indoor obstacles is not addressed by a KITTI-only evaluation.

Yang et al. (2024, Depth Anything V2) and Yang et al. (2024, Depth Anything V1) together establish the training paradigm that makes large-scale zero-shot monocular depth possible: scaling to 62 million unlabeled images for V1 and replacing labeled real images with synthetic data and pseudo-labeled real images for V2. The V2 small variant (25M parameters) is the relevant deployment choice for the Orin Nano. The critical limitation for wheelchair use is that all monocular depth models produce relative (affine-invariant) depth unless explicitly trained or fine-tuned for metric output. Yang et al. (2024, V2) provide metric depth variants, but their indoor scene evaluation focuses on standard benchmarks, not on the sub-4-meter, cluttered-floor, low-camera-height geometry of a wheelchair navigating a hospital corridor. Yu et al. (2024) take this further with ISO, demonstrating full 3D occupancy prediction from a single RGB camera for indoor scenes using the Occ-ScanNet benchmark (40 times the size of NYUv2), but edge deployment latency is entirely uncharacterized — ISO may be too heavy for the Orin Nano's real-time constraint.

The precision wall emerges most concretely from Rustler et al. (2025): the Intel RealSense D435 (hardware structured-light depth) achieves sub-1 cm error at close range, while even the best stereo camera (ZED 2) maintains only 3 cm error at 4 m. Monocular neural depth estimation, even from V2's metric variants, cannot match these hardware depth numbers on a per-frame basis — the fundamental issue is that monocular depth inference is an underdetermined problem that must be resolved by priors learned from training data. For obstacle avoidance where a 20 cm localization error at 1 m could mean the difference between a safe path and a collision, this precision gap is not trivial. The pipeline proposal's honest response is to recommend Level A (RealSense) when the budget allows and treat monocular depth as a Level C fallback requiring calibration anchors. The literature supports that framing but does not provide an empirical characterization of how much safety margin degrades under monocular versus hardware depth.

The unresolved question the literature cannot answer: what is the minimum depth accuracy threshold required for wheelchair obstacle avoidance to be safe, and does Depth Anything V2-small's metric output meet that threshold at indoor ranges (0.5–4 m) when running on the Orin Nano at real-time FPS?

### Theme 2: Stereo Camera Selection Is an Engineering Trade-off with No Dominant Choice — Sensor Capability, GPU Dependency, and Integrated AI Are Competing Axes

For pipeline Level B (stereo without monocular estimation), the sensor choice between RealSense D435, OAK-D Pro, and ZED 2 determines the entire downstream compute architecture. Rustler et al. (2025) provide the only shortlisted paper with a direct empirical comparison of all three, and their findings impose non-trivial design constraints.

The ZED 2 is the most accurate at distance (sub-3 cm at 4 m) but requires an external GPU for depth computation. On the Jetson Orin Nano, this means ZED 2's depth stream consumes significant GPU budget — the same budget needed by YOLOv8, Depth Anything, ORB-SLAM3, and ByteTrack. This is not a marginal cost: Rustler et al. (2025) flag the GPU dependency as a fundamental architectural issue, not a tuning parameter. The OAK-D Pro's integrated AI inference capability (onboard Myriad X VPU) presents the opposite trade: it offloads some neural inference from the Jetson, potentially running lightweight detection or depth estimation natively on the camera, freeing the Orin Nano's GPU for other pipeline stages. The RealSense D435 falls between: hardware structured-light depth (no GPU needed) with sub-1 cm close-range accuracy and a mature ROS driver, but with declining accuracy beyond 2–3 m and known issues with highly reflective or low-texture surfaces.

Lipson et al. (2021, RAFT-Stereo) and Xu et al. (2023, IGEV-Stereo) represent the learned stereo depth alternative: instead of relying on the camera's on-device stereo processing, these algorithms run deep stereo matching on the Orin Nano's GPU to produce higher-quality disparity maps. RAFT-Stereo achieves top performance on the Middlebury indoor benchmark (29% better 1-pixel error than the next-best published method) and ranks highly on ETH3D, which includes indoor scenes. IGEV-Stereo (CVPR 2023) ranks first on KITTI 2015 and 2012 and claims the fastest runtime among the top-10 methods. Both have configurable speed-accuracy trade-offs: RAFT-Stereo through GRU hierarchy depth and IGEV-Stereo through warm-start initialization that reduces the number of ConvGRU iterations needed. The catch is that running learned stereo on the Orin Nano's GPU compounds the GPU budget pressure from YOLOv8 and depth estimation, potentially eliminating the benefit of using a stereo camera at all relative to the RealSense option.

The design tension is therefore not between camera models but between two architectures: (a) RealSense D435 with hardware depth, minimal GPU overhead, and sub-1 cm close-range accuracy; versus (b) stereo camera (OAK-D or ZED) with learned stereo depth (IGEV/RAFT), higher GPU overhead, and potentially superior accuracy at 2–5 m. No shortlisted paper compares these two architectures end-to-end on Jetson-class hardware for indoor mobile robot use. Rustler et al. (2025) compare cameras but not the compute implications of different stereo algorithms on the Orin Nano.

### Theme 3: Visual SLAM for Indoor Wheelchair Navigation Requires a Direct Confrontation with Featureless Environments — the Literature Provides Algorithms but Not the Robustness Data

The SLAM layer replaces 2D LiDAR localization and mapping in the vision-only pipeline. The shortlist's SLAM papers — Campos et al. (2021, ORB-SLAM3) and Labbe and Michaud (2019, RTAB-Map) — are both mature, battle-tested systems with strong indoor evaluation results, but neither was validated in the specific environmental conditions that make wheelchair deployment challenging: long featureless corridors, low-texture painted walls, overhead fluorescent lighting, and a camera mounted at 0.8–1.2 m height rather than the 1.5–1.8 m height used in most SLAM evaluations.

Campos et al. (2021) demonstrate ORB-SLAM3's accuracy comprehensively: stereo-inertial SLAM achieves 3.6 cm average accuracy on EuRoC (drone corridors) and 9 mm accuracy on TUM-VI indoor room sequences. The tracking thread operates at 30–40 FPS, which fits within the pipeline's slow-loop budget when run at reduced frequency (5–10 FPS). ORB-SLAM3's support for monocular, stereo, and RGB-D in a single library is architecturally attractive for the three-level sensor hierarchy. However, ORB-SLAM3 requires sufficient visual features (corners, edges, textures) to maintain localization — a requirement the pipeline proposal explicitly flags as the biggest operational risk for hospital corridors with blank painted walls. The pipeline proposal's mitigation (ArUco fiducial tags on walls) is a known workaround but introduces infrastructure dependency not required by the LiDAR baseline.

Labbe and Michaud (2019) make RTAB-Map the preferred alternative when the output must be a 2D occupancy grid compatible with Nav2's costmap format. RTAB-Map's memory management approach — which limits the set of locations used for loop closure detection to maintain real-time constraints in large-scale environments — is particularly relevant for multi-room wheelchair navigation where a naive graph SLAM approach would grow indefinitely. RTAB-Map is the SLAM library actually deployed in the closest system-level reference paper: Xu et al. (2025, CoNav Chair) use RTAB-Map with the RGB-D sensor as primary input in their ROS-based shared-autonomy wheelchair, demonstrating real indoor corridor navigation with significantly fewer collisions than full-manual or full-autonomous modes. This is the strongest available evidence that RTAB-Map with RGB-D is a viable production choice for the wheelchair context.

Rosinol et al. (2020, Kimera) add a third option: CPU-only real-time metric-semantic 3D mapping from camera and IMU, producing a semantically labeled 3D mesh (floor, walls, furniture categories). CPU-only operation is architecturally significant — it means Kimera can run on the Orin Nano without competing with YOLOv8 and depth estimation for GPU resources. The semantic mesh output enables smarter navigation decisions (e.g., identifying traversable floor versus furniture obstacles) that neither ORB-SLAM3's sparse geometric map nor RTAB-Map's occupancy grid provides. However, Kimera's evaluation datasets and the visual-inertial sensor requirement (camera + IMU) are constraints that must be matched to the wheelchair's sensor configuration.

The open question across all three SLAM systems is robustness under the specific failure modes of wheelchair deployment: sudden stops, rapid turns, dense dynamic obstacle presence (people walking, other wheelchairs), and the low camera height that reduces visible feature density relative to standing-height SLAM evaluations. Ou et al. (2022) address part of this directly: their dynamic SLAM system for visually impaired people demonstrates panoptic segmentation-based dynamic object removal from the SLAM feature set, preventing moving people from corrupting the static map — exactly the dynamic object removal step the pipeline proposal specifies for ORB-SLAM3 and RTAB-Map integration.

### Theme 4: Multi-Object Tracking and Person Re-Identification Are Functionally Separable but Operationally Coupled in Indoor Corridor Navigation

The tracking layer in the vision-only pipeline must handle two distinct but interacting problems: maintaining trajectory continuity for objects that are partially visible or temporarily occluded (multi-object tracking), and recovering identity for objects that leave the camera frame entirely and return (person re-identification). The shortlist covers both problems but from papers designed for different deployment contexts.

Zhang et al. (2022, ByteTrack) solve the occlusion-recovery problem through low-confidence detection association: rather than dropping detections below the confidence threshold, ByteTrack associates weak detections against existing tracklets via IoU similarity, recovering objects that are partially behind door frames or furniture. The 80.3 MOTA, 77.3 IDF1, 63.1 HOTA numbers on MOT17 at 30 FPS on a V100 establish ByteTrack as the benchmark, and its native integration into Ultralytics' `model.track()` API makes it the lowest-friction default. The critical limitation for indoor corridors is that ByteTrack's re-identification after complete disappearance relies on positional prediction by Kalman filter — a mechanism that degrades when a person walks through a doorway and re-enters from an unexpected direction. This is exactly the scenario that the pipeline proposal designates as OC-SORT's preferred use case, but no shortlisted paper provides a head-to-head comparison of ByteTrack versus OC-SORT in a corridor setting.

The person re-ID problem — recovering the same person's identity after they have been outside the camera field of view — is addressed by Syed et al. (2023) from a different angle: a lightweight CNN with part-wise channel and spatial attention, designed for person-following robots and embedded hardware. At 29.4 ms on the Hi3516DV300 embedded camera chip with 88% computation reduction versus full convolutions, this is the only shortlisted re-ID paper with explicit embedded deployment validation. The Rank-1 accuracy numbers (80.73% on PRID, 64.44% on VIPeR) are competitive for a model of this size, but the evaluation datasets (PRID, VIPeR, i-LIDS) were collected in outdoor pedestrian and campus contexts, not indoor hospital corridors. The part-wise attention mechanism — dividing the body into six horizontal bands and learning relationships between them — is explicitly designed to handle viewpoint and illumination variation, which are the dominant factors in indoor corridor re-ID (different lighting zones, varied camera angles as the wheelchair approaches).

The operational coupling between tracking and re-ID becomes critical at longer distances: when a person first appears at 4 m and the wheelchair approaches, YOLO may detect the person with low confidence (small bounding box), ByteTrack must maintain the track through the approach, and when the person turns away or moves behind furniture at 1–2 m, the re-ID mechanism must recover the track. This end-to-end tracking scenario — appearance at distance, approach, brief occlusion, re-identification — is not characterized in any single shortlisted paper. ByteTrack was designed for high-density pedestrian tracking in outdoor or sports contexts; Syed et al. (2023) designed for following a designated companion in mixed environments. Neither paper evaluates the combined use case.

Teed and Deng (2020, RAFT) contribute a complementary motion detection layer: optical flow independent of object detection can flag any moving pixel region, catching dynamic obstacles that YOLO may miss between frames (fast-moving carts, low-profile objects). The RAFT-S variant with 2.7M parameters is deployable at real-time FPS on Jetson-class hardware, and the pipeline proposal calls for running it at 5–10 FPS as a motion mask that feeds into the scene state alongside YOLO detections and ByteTrack tracks. The integration of optical flow, object detection, and tracking has not been characterized in any shortlisted paper as a combined indoor perception system.

### Theme 5: RGB-D Semantic Segmentation for Indoor Scene Analysis Provides Richer Map Representations Than Geometric SLAM — but at GPU Cost

The vision-only pipeline's SLAM layer produces geometric maps (sparse point cloud for ORB-SLAM3, occupancy grid for RTAB-Map). Semantic understanding — knowing that a detected region is a door versus a wall versus a person — enables qualitatively better navigation decisions and is directly relevant to the shared-autonomy context where the LLM slow loop must interpret structured scene descriptions. The shortlist contains two papers that bridge this gap.

Seichter et al. (2021, ESANet) demonstrate efficient RGB-D semantic segmentation optimized for indoor mobile robots via NVIDIA TensorRT on Jetson AGX Xavier (the Ampere architecture family that includes the Orin Nano). Evaluated on NYUv2 and SUNRGB-D — the standard indoor segmentation benchmarks that directly correspond to wheelchair navigation environments — ESANet achieves competitive accuracy while enabling faster inference than contemporary methods. The practical robot deployment demonstration specifically shows free-space identification, person detection, and navigation — the three core semantic capabilities the wheelchair pipeline requires. The TensorRT optimization path documented for Jetson AGX Xavier is directly transferable to the Orin Nano since both use NVIDIA's Ampere architecture, making ESANet a concrete path to on-device semantic segmentation at the needed frequency.

Yu et al. (2024, ISO) take this further from the monocular side: rather than producing per-pixel 2D labels, ISO estimates full 3D occupancy from a single RGB image, leveraging a pretrained depth model as backbone and the D-FLoSP module for 3D voxel feature learning. The Occ-ScanNet benchmark (40 times NYUv2) provides the most relevant indoor evaluation available. The appeal for the wheelchair pipeline is immediate: a 3D occupancy map from a single camera would allow obstacle avoidance without any hardware depth sensor, using the same camera used for YOLO detection. The limitation is that ISO's edge deployment latency is entirely uncharacterized — the paper does not report GPU memory requirements, FPS on Jetson-class hardware, or the inference cost of the D-FLoSP voxel learning module. Given the complexity of 3D voxel processing, ISO likely requires asynchronous, non-real-time map building rather than per-frame inference on the Orin Nano.

Rosinol et al. (2020, Kimera) provide the semantic SLAM variant: by piping semantically labeled images from a segmentation network into the 3D mesh reconstruction module, Kimera produces a metric-semantic 3D mesh with room and furniture labels. The CPU-only operation claim means this could run alongside GPU-heavy YOLO and depth estimation without direct resource competition. The semantic mesh enables the kind of scene understanding the LLM slow loop needs: "door ahead, person at 2 m on the left, navigable floor extends to 3.5 m" — but validating that Kimera's semantic labels are accurate enough for navigation decisions in real indoor environments is not established by any shortlisted paper.

The tension between these approaches (per-pixel 2D segmentation via ESANet, 3D voxel occupancy via ISO, semantic 3D mesh via Kimera) is that each requires different GPU/CPU resources and produces a different output representation. The Nav2 costmap interface ultimately requires an occupancy grid — any of these approaches must be converted to that format, introducing a further processing step. No shortlisted paper compares these three semantic scene understanding approaches head-to-head on the Orin Nano for wheelchair-relevant tasks.

### Theme 6: The Gap Between Academic Benchmarks and Wheelchair-Specific Deployment Conditions Is as Large for Vision-Only as for LiDAR Pipelines

This theme directly mirrors the LiDAR synthesis (Theme 6 of indoor-wheelchair-perception-pipeline/synthesis.md) but takes a different form for the vision-only case. For the LiDAR pipeline, the gap was primarily viewpoint height (sensors designed for standing adult height evaluated on robot-height corridors). For the vision-only pipeline, the gap is threefold: viewpoint height, metric depth calibration, and the indoor/outdoor domain shift in depth estimation training data.

The depth estimation models that dominate the shortlist — Yang et al. (2024, V1 and V2) and Feng et al. (2024) — were trained and evaluated predominantly on outdoor driving data (KITTI) with some indoor benchmarks (NYUv2). The indoor evaluation in these papers uses datasets collected from standard room-scanning hardware at standing height. A wheelchair camera at 0.8–1.2 m sees a dramatically different perspective: more floor, less upper wall, chairs and tables foreshortened at unusual angles, and obstacles like bags, power cables, and wheelchair footrests that are underrepresented in all standard indoor depth estimation benchmarks. Yang et al. (2024, V2) explicitly address domain generalization by training on synthetic data spanning diverse indoor scenes, which partially mitigates this concern — but no shortlisted paper evaluates monocular depth accuracy at wheelchair camera height in indoor environments.

The stereo and SLAM papers share the same viewpoint gap. RAFT-Stereo (Lipson et al., 2021) is evaluated on Middlebury and ETH3D, which include indoor scenes but at conventional camera heights. IGEV-Stereo (Xu et al., 2023) focuses on KITTI outdoor evaluation. ORB-SLAM3 (Campos et al., 2021) reports EuRoC accuracy for drone flight corridors and TUM-VI for room sequences, but drone and handheld camera trajectories differ from wheelchair-height forward-facing motion. RTAB-Map (Labbe and Michaud, 2019) includes TUM RGB-D evaluation, which uses a handheld RGB-D camera at roughly camera-height levels, making it the closest match to the wheelchair scenario — but still not an exact match for the slow, floor-facing trajectory of a powered wheelchair.

Ou et al. (2022) come closest to the target domain: their assistive navigation system for visually impaired people uses an RGB-D sensor carried by a pedestrian, producing a camera height and indoor scene type similar to the wheelchair use case. However, the pedestrian-carried sensor moves faster and more variably than a wheelchair, the dynamic obstacle patterns differ (pedestrian assistant versus wheelchair occupant), and the system is not evaluated on any standard benchmark — qualitative indoor corridor demonstrations substitute for quantitative metrics. The Xu et al. (2025, CoNav Chair) system is the only shortlisted paper that is literally a wheelchair with shared autonomy, RTAB-Map, and RGB-D camera — but it is a preprint with limited quantitative methodology.

The conclusion is that the vision-only pipeline has a more severe deployment gap than the LiDAR baseline: LiDAR depth is sensor-physics-based and transfers well across viewpoints, while monocular depth estimation's learned priors are highly sensitive to the training domain. Closing this gap requires collecting or fine-tuning on data captured at wheelchair camera height in the target indoor environments — a data collection task not mentioned in any shortlisted paper.

## Agreements

**Real-time YOLO inference on Jetson Orin Nano is achievable with TensorRT INT8 quantization.** Alqahtani et al. (2024) confirm the Orin Nano is the fastest tested edge device despite highest idle power. The evaluator note reports 23 ms for YOLOv8n at INT8, fitting within a 50 ms safety loop. This is consistent with the LiDAR pipeline synthesis consensus.

**ByteTrack is the established default tracker for YOLO-paired real-time pipelines.** Zhang et al. (2022) achieve 80.3 MOTA on MOT17 with native Ultralytics integration. No shortlisted paper proposes a drop-in replacement with better speed-accuracy trade-offs at equivalent integration cost. Low-confidence detection association is broadly recognized as essential for tracking partially occluded indoor obstacles.

**RGB-D with hardware depth (RealSense D435) is the most reliable vision-based depth source for 0–4 m indoor ranging.** Rustler et al. (2025) report sub-1 cm error at close range for the D435 and 3 cm at 4 m for the ZED 2. Xu et al. (2025, CoNav Chair) validate the D435 specifically for shared-autonomy wheelchair use. Multiple papers converge on RealSense as the proven indoor robotics depth sensor with mature ROS support.

**RTAB-Map with RGB-D is the established production choice for indoor mobile robot SLAM.** Labbe and Michaud (2019) provide multi-dataset validation including indoor TUM RGB-D; Xu et al. (2025, CoNav Chair) deploy it specifically in a shared-autonomy wheelchair. The memory management approach enabling long-term indoor operation and the native 2D occupancy grid output compatible with Nav2 are consistent advantages across the shortlist.

**Dynamic object removal from SLAM maps is a required design step, not optional.** Ou et al. (2022) demonstrate that incorporating panoptic segmentation to identify and remove dynamic objects from SLAM feature sets prevents map corruption. The pipeline proposal's specification to filter YOLO-detected dynamic objects from the SLAM point cloud is validated by this approach and implicitly required by the Nav2 costmap quality argument from the LiDAR synthesis.

**Optical flow provides independent motion detection complementing classification-based tracking.** Teed and Deng (2020, RAFT) establish that optical flow can flag any moving pixel region regardless of object class. The RAFT-S variant's small parameter count (2.7M) makes it viable as an auxiliary motion sensor on the Orin Nano. The pipeline proposal's motion mask at 5–10 FPS is supported as a practical complement to ByteTrack.

**Edge-only (no-cloud) architecture is operationally necessary for the fast safety loop.** This is consistent with the LiDAR synthesis conclusion. Cloud latency introduces unacceptable variability for real-time obstacle avoidance — all shortlisted system papers that discuss deployment architecture converge on edge-only execution for safety-critical loops.

**Semantic scene understanding enables qualitatively better navigation than geometric mapping alone.** Seichter et al. (2021, ESANet) demonstrate that indoor segmentation (floor, walls, furniture, people) enables free-space identification and navigation on a Jetson AGX Xavier. Rosinol et al. (2020, Kimera) demonstrate that metric-semantic 3D meshes can be produced in real time from camera and IMU. Both papers support the LLM slow loop's requirement for structured semantic scene descriptions beyond raw occupancy grids.

## Contradictions & Debates

**Monocular depth versus hardware depth: accuracy versus sensor cost.** Feng et al. (2024) demonstrate real-time monocular depth inference on Jetson-class hardware. Yang et al. (2024, V2) provide metric depth variants of a foundation model. But Rustler et al. (2025) demonstrate that even the best stereo camera (ZED 2) only achieves 3 cm at 4 m — a standard hardware depth sensor outperforms any reported monocular depth accuracy. The literature does not resolve whether monocular depth at its current accuracy level is sufficient for safe wheelchair obstacle avoidance or merely useful as a fallback when hardware depth fails.

**ORB-SLAM3 versus RTAB-Map: sparse vs. dense, modularity versus Nav2 compatibility.** Campos et al. (2021) demonstrate ORB-SLAM3's superior localization accuracy (3.6 cm on EuRoC) and broader camera modality support. Labbe and Michaud (2019) and Xu et al. (2025) demonstrate RTAB-Map's direct Nav2 costmap compatibility and real indoor wheelchair deployment. These papers do not conflict directly — they occupy different points on the modularity/usability tradeoff — but they lead to incompatible architectural choices. The team must pick one primary SLAM library; the literature supports both without clearly favoring either for the wheelchair-specific use case.

**Learned stereo (RAFT-Stereo, IGEV-Stereo) versus on-device camera stereo (OAK-D, ZED).** Lipson et al. (2021) and Xu et al. (2023, IGEV) demonstrate that learned stereo matching achieves higher accuracy than traditional stereo algorithms. Rustler et al. (2025) show that the ZED 2 achieves competitive real-world indoor accuracy with its on-device processing. The contradiction is that learned stereo on the Orin Nano's GPU competes directly with YOLOv8 and depth estimation for the same compute budget, while on-device stereo (OAK-D's VPU, ZED's CPU stereo) offloads this work at the cost of accuracy. No shortlisted paper characterizes this GPU budget competition empirically for the full concurrent pipeline.

**ISO's 3D occupancy from monocular versus ESANet's 2D semantic segmentation from RGB-D.** Yu et al. (2024) provide 3D volumetric obstacle representation from a single camera — more complete information than 2D segmentation. Seichter et al. (2021) provide validated indoor segmentation at real-time FPS on Jetson AGX Xavier with explicit robot navigation application. ISO's edge deployment feasibility is unknown; ESANet's is validated. The disagreement is not about methodology but about the unknown latency cost of ISO on the Orin Nano. If ISO runs in under 100 ms on the Orin Nano, it dominates ESANet for the slow-loop map-building role; if it requires 500+ ms, it can only update maps asynchronously.

**Kimera's CPU-only semantic SLAM versus ESANet's GPU-optimized 2D segmentation.** Rosinol et al. (2020) claim real-time CPU operation for Kimera's semantic 3D mesh, which would leave the GPU entirely free for YOLO and depth. Seichter et al. (2021) require TensorRT GPU inference but deliver per-frame segmentation rather than a gradually built mesh. These approaches produce different output representations (3D mesh vs. per-pixel 2D labels) that are not directly comparable. The choice between them depends on what the LLM slow loop and Nav2 costmap actually need — a question not addressed by either paper.

**ByteTrack's recall-maximizing low-confidence association versus position-prediction accuracy.** Zhang et al. (2022) demonstrate that associating low-confidence detections recovers occluded objects that threshold-based methods miss. This is directly valuable for wheelchair corridor tracking. However, in low-obstacle-density indoor scenes (1–5 people), false positive track associations from low-confidence background regions could cause the wheelchair to avoid phantom obstacles. The ByteTrack paper was evaluated on high-density pedestrian benchmarks (MOT17, MOT20) where the false positive cost is lower. The wheelchair's indoor sparse scene with high safety stakes for false negatives (missed person) versus false positives (phantom obstacle) may require different confidence thresholds than the default MOT17-optimized setting. No shortlisted paper characterizes ByteTrack's behavior at low obstacle density.

**Person re-ID accuracy on indoor domain versus evaluation benchmark domain.** Syed et al. (2023) report 80.73% Rank-1 on PRID and 64.44% on VIPeR — datasets from outdoor pedestrian and campus contexts. Indoor hospital corridor re-ID involves different lighting (fluorescent overhead vs. daylight), different clothing diversity (medical staff uniforms), and different motion patterns (slower, more stop-and-start). The accuracy numbers from outdoor re-ID benchmarks are not directly predictive of indoor wheelchair corridor performance.

## Open Questions & Research Gaps

**What is the metric depth accuracy of Depth Anything V2-small on the Orin Nano at wheelchair-relevant indoor ranges (0.5–4 m)?** This is the single most important empirical gap for the vision-only pipeline. All evidence for monocular depth accuracy in the shortlist comes from KITTI (outdoor driving) or standard indoor benchmarks at standing height. No paper evaluates metric depth at wheelchair camera height (0.8–1.2 m) in hospital or assisted living corridors. This experiment is directly publishable.

**What is the compound latency of the full vision-only stack (YOLO + ByteTrack + depth + ORB-SLAM3/RTAB-Map) running concurrently on the Jetson Orin Nano?** Every shortlisted benchmark measures components in isolation. Feng et al. (2024) measure RT-MonoDepth-S alone. Alqahtani et al. (2024) measure YOLOv8 alone. Zhang et al. (2022) measure ByteTrack on a V100. Campos et al. (2021) measure ORB-SLAM3 tracking at 30–40 FPS on a workstation. GPU contention when these stages run concurrently on the Orin Nano's shared memory bus is not characterized anywhere in the literature. This is the team's primary engineering measurement gap.

**Does ORB-SLAM3 maintain stable localization in featureless indoor corridors (blank painted walls, uniform floors)?** Campos et al. (2021) evaluate on EuRoC (drone corridors with rich texture) and TUM-VI (indoor rooms with furniture and varied surfaces). Neither dataset contains the long, uniform corridors typical of hospitals or care facilities. The pipeline proposal explicitly flags this as an operational risk and suggests ArUco tags as mitigation, but no shortlisted paper quantifies how quickly ORB-SLAM3 loses localization in low-feature environments or how many ArUco tags per meter are needed to maintain stability.

**Is ISO's 3D occupancy prediction deployable at real-time or near-real-time FPS on the Jetson Orin Nano?** Yu et al. (2024) provide accuracy numbers on NYUv2 and Occ-ScanNet but no latency or memory characterization on edge hardware. Given the D-FLoSP module's 3D voxel feature learning, the compute cost is likely substantially higher than 2D segmentation. Whether ISO can run at even 5 FPS (suitable for asynchronous map building) on the Orin Nano is unknown.

**How does Depth Anything V2-small perform on low-camera-height indoor scenes with floor-level obstacles (bags, power cables, wheelchair footrests)?** These are underrepresented in all training datasets — both outdoor driving data (KITTI) and indoor room scanning (NYUv2, ScanNet). Floor-level obstacles are the highest-risk detection failure mode for wheelchair navigation. The pipeline proposal acknowledges this as the weakest point of camera-only versus LiDAR detection (the LiDAR synthesis identified floor-level coverage as LiDAR's advantage). No shortlisted paper quantifies how monocular depth estimates degrade for floor-plane objects at low camera height.

**Can ByteTrack's default confidence thresholds be safely applied in the wheelchair's sparse indoor obstacle scene?** Zhang et al. (2022) calibrate ByteTrack for high-density pedestrian tracking benchmarks. The wheelchair's indoor environment has 1–5 moving obstacles with different density, occlusion patterns, and false positive risk profiles. The safety implications of track fragmentation (missed person) versus false track association (phantom obstacle) are asymmetric in the wheelchair context but not characterized for sparse scenes in any shortlisted paper.

**What is the minimum viable metric depth accuracy for wheelchair obstacle avoidance, and does it differ by obstacle type?** No shortlisted paper, and no paper in the adjacent literature, defines a safety threshold for depth accuracy in wheelchair obstacle avoidance. Is ±20 cm at 2 m sufficient to plan a safe path around a person? Does the acceptable error scale with distance? These questions define the success criterion for the depth estimation module but are not established in the literature.

**How does the vision-only pipeline's detection recall degrade in low-light indoor conditions?** All depth estimation papers assume adequate illumination. RGB-based YOLO detection degrades in low-light (cafeteria evening, patient room with reduced lighting). Unlike LiDAR, which works in darkness, camera pipelines have an illumination dependency not characterized by any shortlisted paper for indoor wheelchair-relevant scenarios.

**What scene representation format best serves the LLM slow loop?** The pipeline proposal's structured JSON scene state message is proposed without empirical validation. Neither the tracking papers (Zhang et al., 2022; Syed et al., 2023) nor the SLAM papers (Campos et al., 2021; Labbe and Michaud, 2019) address how to represent dynamic scene state for language model consumption. This is a gap shared with the LiDAR synthesis and is joint work with the LLM teammates.

**How does RTAB-Map's memory management perform during a full clinical day of operation (6–8 hours) in a multi-room environment?** Labbe and Michaud (2019) validate long-term operation on the MIT Stata Center dataset (a large building), which is encouraging. The wheelchair-specific question is whether the memory bounds prevent map degradation during a shift-length deployment with recurring room visits (patient returns to the same room multiple times). This operational duration stress test is not addressed by any shortlisted paper.

## Methodological Notes

**Dominant methods across the shortlist.** Algorithm papers (Feng et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024 V1 and V2; Lipson et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2023 IGEV; Zhang et al., 2022; Teed and Deng, 2020; Yu et al., 2024) report benchmark comparison results against established leaderboards (KITTI, Middlebury, ETH3D, MOT17, NYUv2, Occ-ScanNet). System papers (Campos et al., 2021; Labbe and Michaud, 2019; Seichter et al., 2021; Rosinol et al., 2020; Ou et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2025 CoNav Chair) validate complete pipelines on multiple datasets or physical deployments. Hardware benchmark papers (Alqahtani et al., 2024; Rustler et al., 2025) measure defined metrics (latency, energy, depth error) across hardware configurations. The person re-ID paper (Syed et al., 2023) follows classification benchmark methodology on standard re-ID datasets.

**Hardware generation heterogeneity.** Feng et al. (2024) test on Jetson Nano and Jetson AGX Orin (neither is the Orin Nano). Seichter et al. (2021) test on Jetson AGX Xavier. Alqahtani et al. (2024) test on Jetson Orin Nano (direct match) but for detection only. Zhang et al. (2022) use a V100 server GPU. No shortlisted paper measures ORB-SLAM3, RTAB-Map, IGEV-Stereo, RAFT-Stereo, or ISO on the Orin Nano specifically. All performance claims for these components on the target hardware are extrapolations, not measured facts.

**Indoor benchmark coverage.** NYUv2 and SUNRGB-D are the dominant indoor depth/segmentation benchmarks (used by Seichter et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2024 V2; Yu et al., 2024). TUM RGB-D is the indoor SLAM benchmark (evaluated in Labbe and Michaud, 2019). ETH3D includes indoor scenes for stereo evaluation (Lipson et al., 2021). MOT17 and MOT20 are outdoor or surveillance-height tracking benchmarks — their indoor coverage is minimal. The evaluation ecosystems for depth estimation and tracking are largely non-overlapping, making cross-component accuracy claims difficult to validate against a single ground truth.

**Depth evaluation metric inconsistency.** Depth estimation papers report relative error metrics on KITTI (AbsRel, SqRel, RMSE); stereo papers report 1-pixel or 3-pixel error on Middlebury/KITTI; hardware comparison papers (Rustler et al., 2025) report absolute error in centimeters for specific distance ranges. These metrics are not directly comparable. Converting across them to answer "is this good enough for wheelchair safety" requires calibration experiments that no shortlisted paper provides.

**Geographic and operational deployment gap.** No shortlisted paper evaluates any component of the vision-only pipeline in an actual power wheelchair deployment, hospital corridor, or care facility. Xu et al. (2025, CoNav Chair) come closest, but as a preprint with limited quantitative results. The operational domain transfer from lab and outdoor benchmarks to wheelchair clinical deployment is an inference across all 17 papers.

**Venue quality range.** Top-tier: Yang et al. (2024 V2) at NeurIPS 2024; Zhang et al. (2022) at ECCV 2022; Teed and Deng (2020) at ECCV 2020 (Best Paper); Yang et al. (2024 V1) at CVPR 2024; Xu et al. (2023 IGEV) at CVPR 2023; Campos et al. (2021) in IEEE TRO; Labbe and Michaud (2019) in Journal of Field Robotics; Yu et al. (2024) at ECCV 2024; Rosinol et al. (2020) at ICRA 2020; Seichter et al. (2021) at ICRA 2021; Lipson et al. (2021) at 3DV 2021. Mid-tier: Rustler et al. (2025) in IEEE Access; Ou et al. (2022) at ICCHP 2022; Syed et al. (2023) in MDPI Sensors; Alqahtani et al. (2024) as preprint. Preprint: Xu et al. (2025, CoNav Chair); Feng et al. (2024, ICIP 2024 accepted but arXiv hosted). The preprints provide the highest topical specificity (edge deployment, wheelchair systems) but lowest result confidence.

**Contrast with LiDAR pipeline synthesis.** The LiDAR synthesis (indoor-wheelchair-perception-pipeline/synthesis.md) identified seven papers providing direct Jetson Orin Nano benchmarks across detection, tracking, and LiDAR perception — covering more of the hardware benchmark gap. The vision-only synthesis has only one paper (Alqahtani et al., 2024) with a direct Orin Nano detection benchmark, and zero papers with Orin Nano benchmarks for depth estimation, stereo matching, or visual SLAM. The vision-only pipeline therefore carries greater empirical uncertainty in its performance projections than the LiDAR baseline.

## Recommended Narrative Arc for Report

The report should open with the architectural constraint (vision-only, Jetson Orin Nano) and immediately establish the sensor tier system as the organizing framework, since the entire pipeline branches on sensor choice. It should then work through algorithmic components in pipeline order (depth → detection → tracking → SLAM → scene semantics), close with the deployment gap analysis, and frame the team's experimental contributions as closing specific gaps that the literature has left open.

1. **Open with the sensor tier decision as the architectural pivot.** Establish that the vision-only constraint produces three fundamentally different pipelines (Level A: RealSense hardware depth; Level B: stereo with learned matching; Level C: monocular neural depth), and that Rustler et al. (2025) provide the empirical grounding for sensor selection. Frame the ZED 2 GPU dependency finding and the OAK-D AI integration finding as hardware design constraints, not preferences.

2. **Introduce the depth estimation layer as the pipeline-defining component.** Use Feng et al. (2024) as the edge deployment baseline for monocular depth, Yang et al. (2024 V2) as the accuracy ceiling and metric depth approach, and Lipson et al. (2021) / Xu et al. (2023 IGEV) as the learned stereo alternatives. Present the precision wall clearly: hardware depth (Rustler et al., 2025) outperforms neural depth in accuracy; neural depth's value is in sensor cost, not depth quality.

3. **Cover detection and tracking as the pipeline's latency-critical core.** Use Alqahtani et al. (2024) to anchor the Orin Nano latency budget for YOLOv8, Zhang et al. (2022) to establish ByteTrack as the default tracker, and Syed et al. (2023) to introduce the person re-ID layer for longer-term identity maintenance. Frame Teed and Deng (2020, RAFT) as the complementary motion detection that catches non-YOLO-detected dynamic obstacles.

4. **Address the SLAM layer as the largest architectural difference from the LiDAR pipeline.** Lead with Xu et al. (2025, CoNav Chair) as evidence that RTAB-Map + RGB-D works for shared-autonomy wheelchair navigation. Use Campos et al. (2021, ORB-SLAM3) to establish the accuracy ceiling for visual SLAM and the modality flexibility. Use Labbe and Michaud (2019, RTAB-Map) for the Nav2 integration argument. Introduce Ou et al. (2022) to motivate dynamic object removal from SLAM maps.

5. **Introduce semantic scene understanding as the bridge to the LLM slow loop.** Use Seichter et al. (2021, ESANet) for the validated GPU-accelerated indoor segmentation path; Rosinol et al. (2020, Kimera) for the CPU-only semantic mesh alternative; and Yu et al. (2024, ISO) for the monocular occupancy estimation research frontier. Frame these not as production choices but as options with different GPU/CPU trade-offs and uncharacterized latency costs on the Orin Nano.

6. **Close with the deployment gap analysis.** Establish that the vision-only pipeline's largest unresolved questions cluster around three measurements the team can make: (a) monocular depth accuracy at wheelchair height in indoor corridors, (b) compound pipeline latency on the Orin Nano, and (c) ORB-SLAM3 or RTAB-Map localization stability in low-texture indoor environments. Frame these as directly publishable experimental contributions that the existing literature has not addressed.
