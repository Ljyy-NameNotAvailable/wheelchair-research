# Shared Autonomy in Power Wheelchairs: A Systematic Literature Review

**Date:** 2026-04-03
**Topic:** Shared Autonomy in Power Wheelchairs
**Papers reviewed:** 15

---

## Abstract

This review synthesizes fifteen peer-reviewed papers and theses on shared autonomy in powered wheelchairs, spanning foundational algorithmic frameworks through recent empirical evaluations. Shared autonomy — the collaborative distribution of control authority between a human operator and an autonomous system — has emerged as the dominant paradigm for assistive mobility robotics, positioned between purely manual teleoperation and full autonomy. The literature converges on several robust findings: shared autonomy consistently reduces collisions and cognitive load compared to unassisted manual control; intent inference under uncertainty outperforms strategies that delay assistance until goal identification is confident; and no single control paradigm is universally optimal across users, interfaces, or populations. Equally important are the field's unresolved tensions: the static-user assumption embedded in most algorithmic frameworks is empirically challenged; model-based and model-free intent inference families have not been directly compared in wheelchair contexts with clinical populations; and ecological validity has declined in recent work even as algorithmic sophistication has advanced. Critical gaps include the near-complete absence of longitudinal studies, underrepresentation of diverse clinical populations, lack of standardized evaluation benchmarks, and uncharted pathways from prototype systems to regulatory approval.

## 1. Themes

### 1.1 Intent Inference as the Central Algorithmic Challenge

The problem of inferring user intent from noisy, ambiguous control signals sits at the heart of every shared autonomy framework in this literature. Demeester et al. (2008) established the foundational template: model user intent probabilistically using a Bayesian framework, represent uncertainty about that intent explicitly, and use that uncertainty to govern how aggressively the system intervenes. Their greedy Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP) formulation, validated with both healthy subjects and participants with Cerebral Palsy, produced approximately ten percent faster task completion than local obstacle avoidance systems while accommodating user-specific driving profiles. This template was generalized and theoretically refined by Javdani et al. (2018), who demonstrated that the canonical predict-then-act strategy — await confident goal identification before providing assistance — is structurally flawed: by the time confidence is high, the user has nearly completed the task without help. Their hindsight optimization approach instead minimizes expected cost-to-go simultaneously across all plausible goals, providing assistance throughout the task rather than only near its completion. The empirical advantages of this formulation — faster goal achievement, less user input required, fewer collisions — were demonstrated in teleoperation and teaming scenarios and have since propagated through the wheelchair-specific literature. Ghorbel et al. (2017) directly applied the POMDP shared autonomy logic to the SmartWheeler clinical platform, confirming that goal-uncertainty-aware action selection reduces cognitive workload in real driving scenarios with eight participants. Carlson and Demiris (2012) provided complementary empirical evidence in a wheelchair context, demonstrating that prediction-guided intent inference eliminates collisions that occur under unassisted driving while simultaneously reducing cognitive load — validated through both eye-tracking and secondary-task analysis, establishing methodological tools that subsequent work has adopted. Across all algorithmic contributions in this set, the shared premise is that better probabilistic intent models produce better assistance — an assumption that Aronson and Short (2024) subsequently subject to empirical scrutiny.

### 1.2 Autonomy Level as a Design Variable, Not a Binary Choice

A consistent empirical finding across the literature is that "how much autonomy to provide" is neither a binary decision nor a universal optimum to be determined once and applied globally. Erdogan and Argall (2017) conducted one of the most methodologically rigorous comparative studies in the field, implementing four distinct control-sharing paradigms and evaluating them across multiple sessions with participants including individuals with spinal cord injury (SCI). Their key finding — that higher autonomy generally improves task performance and reduces user effort but that no single paradigm is universally superior — establishes autonomy level as a design parameter that must be individualized. The interactions between autonomy level, interface type, and user characteristics were sufficiently complex that a one-size-fits-all prescription was not possible, and the authors explicitly recommend that systems offer multiple control options to accommodate individual requirements. Boucher et al. (2013) demonstrated that high autonomy can be clinically acceptable even against a demanding baseline: their SmartWheeler navigation system reduced collisions by more than sixty percent compared to prior intelligent wheelchair platforms while incurring only a four percent performance penalty relative to experienced joystick users — despite participants having minimal training on the intelligent system versus years of joystick experience. Xu et al. (2025) replicated this tradeoff pattern in a more recent system evaluated across three modes with twenty-one participants, finding that shared control produced the fewest collisions while matching autonomous and manual modes on completion time and trajectory efficiency. Guo et al. (2025) add a cognitive dimension to this picture, finding that higher automation levels markedly reduce cognitive load and that progressive information presentation reduces reaction times by 23.5 percent across fifty-two participants in simulation. The critical counterpoint is supplied by Viswanathan et al. (2017), who found that older adults with cognitive impairment specifically and consistently preferred to remain in the control loop, demonstrating that optimal autonomy level is population-specific and cannot be determined from aggregate performance metrics alone. Argall (2018) frames this individualization challenge as one of the defining open problems for the rehabilitation robotics field, noting that the gap between what robotic technology can do and what assistive robotics has delivered is partly explained by insufficient attention to the heterogeneity of user needs and preferences.

### 1.3 The Static-User Assumption and User-Centered Design

The dominant algorithmic paradigm across the shortlisted literature treats user behavior as a fixed signal to be interpreted: intent inference systems observe what the user does and infer what they want, then modify the robot's trajectory accordingly. The user's own behavior is assumed to remain constant regardless of whether assistance is active. Aronson and Short (2024) directly and empirically challenge this assumption in two studies — a computer-based bubble-popping task and a public-space robot control study — demonstrating that users intentionally adapt their control strategies when assistance is active and describe these changes as deliberate responses to altered system dynamics. Users in the assisted condition were not benefiting from the same behavior being redirected; they were behaving differently. This has cascading methodological implications: evaluation studies that compare performance with and without assistance may be measuring user strategy change as a confound rather than isolating the contribution of the assistance itself. It also has algorithmic implications: intent models calibrated on unassisted user behavior will systematically misinterpret behavior exhibited when assistance is active. Viswanathan et al. (2017) reinforce the user-centered argument from a preferences perspective, finding that cognitively impaired older adults have specific, articulable preferences about when autonomous assistance is appropriate and that these preferences would not be predicted from general autonomy research. Urdiales et al. (2011) operationalize a user-centered philosophy through a continuous efficiency-weighting approach in which control authority blends dynamically based on the comparative moment-to-moment performance of user and system, rather than a fixed allocation — acknowledging that the user's contribution has independent value that should be preserved rather than overridden. Tested with eighteen volunteers with varying physical and cognitive disabilities in a home setting, this approach produced statistically significant performance improvements (p = 0.016), and both users and caregivers provided positive feedback. Carlson and Demiris (2012) established the measurement toolkit for evaluating these user-centered outcomes, combining eye-tracking with secondary task analysis to quantify cognitive workload reduction — a methodological contribution that has shaped how the field operationalizes user benefit.

### 1.4 Learning-Based Methods and the Move Toward Model-Free Frameworks

Early shared autonomy approaches for powered wheelchairs relied on hand-coded intent models, pre-defined goal sets, and structured representations of the environment that required domain expertise to construct (Demeester et al., 2008; Carlson and Demiris, 2012). A major methodological shift, visible across the shortlisted literature's timeline, is the move toward learned, model-free approaches that remove these brittleness-inducing dependencies. Reddy et al. (2018) proposed a deep reinforcement learning framework for shared autonomy that requires no prior knowledge of environmental dynamics, the set of possible user goals, or the user's policy given their goal — learning an end-to-end mapping from environmental observation and user input to assistive action values, using task reward as the sole supervisory signal. Evaluated with twelve participants on a video game task and four participants flying a physical quadrotor, the framework demonstrated that a model-free agent can implicitly infer user intent from input alone and improve task completion over both unassisted users and fully autonomous agents. This approach substantially expands applicability to real-world scenarios where goal sets cannot be enumerated in advance — a critical limitation of POMDP-based frameworks in the ecological complexity of community mobility. Chatzidimitriadis (2023) translates this paradigm directly to powered wheelchair navigation, developing a mapless reinforcement learning navigation system trained in virtual environments and a shared-control RL framework that accepts noisy user input while maintaining safety. The combined system, integrated with a vision-based head-control interface, achieved a ninety-two percent reduction in collisions in real-world testing scenarios. The trajectory from structured probabilistic models to model-free deep RL reflects a field-wide recognition that clinical environments — with their spatial variability, diverse user populations, and unpredictable dynamics — are too complex for purely hand-engineered solutions. Argall (2018) frames this evolution as the necessary direction for rehabilitation robotics, arguing that systems must perceive the external world and reason about high-level control decisions to meaningfully reduce operational demands on users with severe impairments.

### 1.5 Clinical Validity and the Simulation-to-Reality Gap

A persistent and structurally important tension runs through the shortlisted literature, separating papers with high algorithmic sophistication from those with high ecological validity. Boucher et al. (2013) established the ecological validity benchmark for the field: seventeen subjects, thirty-two sessions totaling twenty-five hours of operation and nine kilometers of driving, culminating in the introduction of the Robotic Wheelchair Skills Test as a standardized evaluation protocol. Urdiales et al. (2011) provided a complementary benchmark in home-environment testing with eighteen volunteers with actual disabilities, reporting statistically significant results across a heterogeneous clinical population. These studies represent the high-water mark of ecological validity in the shortlist. The contrast with recent work is striking: Guo et al. (2025) deployed the largest sample in the set — fifty-two participants — but evaluated exclusively in a Unity-based digital twin simulation, with no physical hardware and no participants with disabilities. Xu et al. (2025) employed a physical robotic platform in a real indoor environment but recruited only twenty-one unimpaired adult participants. Reddy et al. (2018), whose algorithmic contribution is seminal, validated with twelve participants on a video game and four on a quadrotor — no wheelchair users, no clinical population. Chatzidimitriadis (2023) explicitly confronted the simulation-to-reality gap, training a navigation system in virtual environments and then validating against real-world scenarios, but the population and environment scope remained limited. How et al. (2013) offer a methodologically instructive middle ground: a two-phase evaluation combining controlled laboratory performance testing with user trials involving cognitively impaired seniors in a realistic long-term care setting, and notably reporting failure modes alongside positive findings — audio prompt non-adherence and minimal improvement in task completion time — a transparency that increases the credibility of the positive results reported elsewhere. The field faces a structural problem: the studies with the largest samples and most sophisticated evaluation methodologies tend not to involve clinical populations, while the studies with clinical populations tend to have small samples and limited sessions.

### 1.6 Population Specificity and the Heterogeneity of Clinical Need

The powered wheelchair user population encompasses individuals with diverse motor, cognitive, and sensory impairments, and the shared autonomy literature has not yet adequately grappled with the implications of this heterogeneity. Demeester et al. (2008) included both healthy subjects and participants with Cerebral Palsy but did not quantify the differential benefit between groups. Erdogan and Argall (2017) directly compared SCI participants with uninjured controls and found that the two groups diverged in subjective preferences even when objective performance was similar, providing empirical evidence that uninjured proxy populations cannot be assumed to represent clinical users without verification. Viswanathan et al. (2017) focused specifically on older adults with cognitive impairment — a population with qualitatively different needs from those with motor-only impairments — and identified preferences and concerns that would not have been predicted from general autonomy research conducted with other populations. How et al. (2013) identified a failure mode specific to this same population: cognitively impaired participants showed suboptimal adherence to audio navigation prompts, a limitation that would not appear in samples of neurotypical adults and that has direct implications for interface design. Xu et al. (2025) and Guo et al. (2025), representing the most recent empirical contributions in the set, evaluated exclusively with unimpaired participants — a regression in ecological validity even as system sophistication has advanced. Argall (2018) identifies the development of customized, individualized assistance as a defining challenge for the rehabilitation robotics community, noting that both motor impairments and the interfaces used to communicate with assistive systems vary enormously across users. The literature as a whole is disproportionately weighted toward SCI and cognitively impaired older adults; individuals with ALS, multiple sclerosis, traumatic brain injury, and progressive neuromuscular diseases are largely absent from the evidence base.

## 2. Key Papers

**Javdani et al. (2018)** — [Shared autonomy via hindsight optimization for teleoperation and teaming](https://doi.org/10.1177/0278364918776060)
*The International Journal of Robotics Research, 2018*
This paper formalizes shared autonomy as a POMDP and introduces hindsight optimization as a tractable approximation, replacing the predict-then-act paradigm with assistance that acts under goal uncertainty from the start of a task. Evaluated in both teleoperation and human-robot teaming scenarios, the method achieves goals faster, requires less user input, and produces fewer collisions than predict-then-act alternatives. Its theoretical critique of confident-prediction-first strategies and its POMDP formulation are foundational references for virtually all subsequent work in shared autonomy for assistive robotics.

**Erdogan and Argall (2017)** — [The effect of robotic wheelchair control paradigm and interface on user performance, effort and preference: An experimental assessment](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.robot.2017.04.013)
*Robotics and Autonomous Systems, 2017*
This study compares four distinct control-sharing paradigms across multiple sessions with participants including individuals with spinal cord injury, using multiple interfaces — one of the most rigorous comparative evaluations in the wheelchair shared autonomy literature. Higher autonomy levels consistently improve task performance and reduce user effort, but no single paradigm is universally superior, and subjective preferences diverge between SCI participants and uninjured controls. The explicit conclusion that multiple control options must be available to accommodate individual users is a direct challenge to one-size-fits-all system design.

**Aronson and Short (2024)** — [Intentional User Adaptation to Shared Control Assistance](https://doi.org/10.1145/3610977.3634953)
*ACM/IEEE International Conference on Human-Robot Interaction (HRI), 2024*
Through a two-study design — a computer-based bubble-popping task and a public-space robot control study — this paper demonstrates that users intentionally change their control strategies when shared control assistance is active and describe these changes as deliberate adaptations to altered system dynamics. The finding directly undermines the static-user assumption that underpins most shared autonomy intent models. This work motivates a fundamental rethinking of evaluation methodology and algorithm design in assistive shared control.

**Argall (2018)** — [Autonomy in Rehabilitation Robotics: An Intersection](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-control-061417-041727)
*Annual Review of Control, Robotics, and Autonomous Systems, 2018*
This authoritative survey by the leading researcher in rehabilitation robotics shared control surveys the full landscape of autonomy in prosthetics, wheelchairs, and robotic arms, framing shared control, intent inference, and variable autonomy as the core challenge triad. The paper argues that robotic systems capable of high-level perceptual reasoning could substantially reduce operational demands on severely impaired users and bridges the clinical and algorithmic research communities. It identifies individualized assistance and the often-overlooked clinical considerations as the defining open problems for the field.

**Boucher et al. (2013)** — [Design and validation of an intelligent wheelchair towards a clinically-functional outcome](https://doi.org/10.1186/1743-0003-10-58)
*Journal of NeuroEngineering and Rehabilitation, 2013*
The SmartWheeler platform evaluation sets the ecological validity benchmark for the field: seventeen subjects, thirty-two sessions, nine kilometers of operation, and a decision-theoretic speech-control system. Collision frequency was reduced by more than sixty percent relative to prior intelligent wheelchair platforms, with only a four percent performance penalty compared to experienced joystick users. The paper introduces the Robotic Wheelchair Skills Test, providing the field's most developed standardized evaluation protocol.

**Demeester et al. (2008)** — [User-adapted plan recognition and user-adapted shared control: A Bayesian approach to semi-autonomous wheelchair driving](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10514-007-9064-5)
*Autonomous Robots, 2008*
This paper establishes the template for intent-inference-based shared control in wheelchair robotics: a Bayesian framework that models complex user intent, explicitly represents uncertainty, incorporates user-specific parameters, and uses a greedy POMDP for decision-making. Tested with healthy subjects and participants with Cerebral Palsy, the system achieves approximately ten percent faster task completion than local obstacle avoidance systems. Its user-adapted formulation and empirical inclusion of a clinical population make it the foundational reference for wheelchair-specific shared autonomy research.

**Reddy et al. (2018)** — [Shared Autonomy via Deep Reinforcement Learning](https://doi.org/10.15607/RSS.2018.XIV.005)
*Robotics: Science and Systems (RSS), 2018*
This paper introduces a model-free deep reinforcement learning framework for shared autonomy that removes prior knowledge requirements about environmental dynamics, user goals, and user policy — learning an end-to-end mapping from observation and user input to assistive action values using only task reward. Evaluated with twelve participants on a video game task and four on a real quadrotor, the framework improves task completion over both unassisted users and fully autonomous agents. It is the enabling reference for model-free approaches to wheelchair shared autonomy.

**Carlson and Demiris (2012)** — [Collaborative control for a robotic wheelchair: evaluation of performance, attention, and workload](https://doi.org/10.1109/TSMCB.2011.2181833)
*IEEE Transactions on Systems, Man, and Cybernetics, Part B (Cybernetics), 2012*
This study demonstrates that prediction-guided collaborative wheelchair control eliminates collisions that occur under unassisted driving while simultaneously reducing cognitive workload, assessed through both eye-tracking and secondary task analysis. The combination of performance measurement with physiological and dual-task cognitive load measures establishes the methodological template for evaluating shared autonomy benefits in assistive contexts. The paper is particularly influential for its application to brain-machine interface scenarios where user input is low-frequency and noisy.

**Ghorbel et al. (2017)** — [A decision-theoretic approach for the collaborative control of a smart wheelchair](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12369-017-0434-7)
*International Journal of Social Robotics, 2017*
This paper bridges the theoretical shared autonomy literature (Javdani et al., 2018) and the clinical SmartWheeler platform, implementing POMDP-based collaborative control with eight participants and demonstrating reduced cognitive workload in real wheelchair navigation. The system's two-component architecture — a prediction module estimating user goals from sensor and input data, and an assistance module minimizing expected user cost — operationalizes the goal-uncertainty-aware action selection framework in a clinically deployed platform. Co-authored by Javdani and Srinivasa, it directly connects the algorithmic and applied communities.

**Urdiales et al. (2011)** — [Wheelchair collaborative control for disabled users navigating indoors](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.artmed.2011.05.002)
*Artificial Intelligence in Medicine, 2011*
This paper operationalizes a user-centered philosophy through continuous efficiency-weighting blending, in which control authority is allocated dynamically to user or system based on their comparative moment-to-moment navigation performance, rather than using a fixed allocation or mode-switching. Tested with eighteen volunteers with varying physical and cognitive disabilities in a home setting, the approach produced statistically significant performance improvements (p = 0.016) alongside positive caregiver feedback. Its inclusion of a heterogeneous clinical population in an ecologically realistic home environment is a methodological benchmark rarely matched in later work.

**Viswanathan et al. (2017)** — [Intelligent wheelchair control strategies for older adults with cognitive impairment: user attitudes, needs, and preferences](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10514-016-9568-y)
*Autonomous Robots, 2017*
This study empirically reveals that older adults with cognitive impairment consistently want to remain in the control loop during wheelchair operation, and articulate specific contexts where autonomous assistance is and is not appropriate — findings that directly challenge fully autonomous approaches and that would not be predicted from research on other populations. The mock intelligent wheelchair protocol used to elicit preferences offers a methodological model for user-centered design research in assistive robotics. The paper fills a critical gap by treating a population with distinct cognitive characteristics as the primary design constituency.

**How et al. (2013)** — [Evaluation of an intelligent wheelchair system for older adults with cognitive impairments](https://doi.org/10.1186/1743-0003-10-90)
*Journal of NeuroEngineering and Rehabilitation, 2013*
This two-phase evaluation combines laboratory performance testing against realistic long-term care obstacles with user trials involving cognitively impaired seniors, finding that the system reduces collision frequency and lowers perceived workload. Notably, the paper also reports failure modes: audio prompt adherence was suboptimal in some participants and task completion time showed minimal improvement — transparently reported limitations that increase the credibility of the positive findings. The population-specific failure mode identified here (audio non-adherence) would be invisible in neurotypical samples and carries direct implications for interface design.

**Xu et al. (2025)** — [CoNav Chair: Development and Evaluation of a Shared Control based Wheelchair for the Built Environment](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.11716)
*arXiv preprint, 2025*
The CoNav Chair implements a ROS-based shared control navigation framework and evaluates it in three modes — manual, shared, and autonomous — with twenty-one unimpaired participants in a real indoor building environment. Shared control produced significantly fewer collisions than either manual or autonomous modes, while matching both on completion time, trajectory efficiency, and smoothness — and was rated by users as safer and more efficient. As the most recent physical wheelchair shared-control evaluation in the shortlist, it provides a current state-of-the-art performance baseline, though its limitation to unimpaired participants constrains clinical generalizability.

**Guo et al. (2025)** — [Shared autonomy for cognitive load regulation in autonomous wheelchairs](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10111-025-00825-6)
*Cognition, Technology & Work, 2025*
This study introduces a cognitive digital twin framework, deploying Unity-based simulation experiments with fifty-two participants — the largest sample in the shortlisted set — to evaluate multi-level human-machine interface designs for shared and supervisory wheelchair autonomy. Progressive information presentation reduced reaction times by 23.5 percent and improved task accuracy to 98.1 percent; higher automation levels markedly enhanced user experience and operational efficiency while lowering cognitive load. The simulation-based methodology is a limitation for direct clinical translation, but the HMI design findings and specific cognitive load quantification are directly applicable to real-world wheelchair interface design.

**Chatzidimitriadis (2023)** — [Reinforcement Learning for Shared Autonomy in Powered Wheelchair Navigation](https://doi.org/10.22024/UniKent/01.02.102477)
*University of Kent (PhD thesis), 2023*
This doctoral thesis develops three contributions to RL-based wheelchair shared autonomy: a mapless autonomous navigation system trained in virtual environments and validated against established path planning methods; a novel shared control RL framework that accepts noisy user input while maintaining safety; and a vision-based head-control interface using face detection as an alternative input modality. The combined system achieves a ninety-two percent reduction in collisions in real-world testing scenarios, demonstrating the practical viability of model-free RL for wheelchair navigation. As a thesis rather than a peer-reviewed journal article, rigor standards differ, but the direct translation of deep RL shared autonomy to a powered wheelchair platform with real-world validation fills a critical implementation gap.

## 3. Contradictions and Debates

**Predict-Then-Act Versus Action Under Uncertainty**

The most fundamental algorithmic debate in the shortlisted literature concerns when shared autonomy systems should begin providing assistance. Javdani et al. (2018) mount a theoretical and empirical argument that predict-then-act shared autonomy is structurally inferior: because confident goal predictions typically arrive only when the user has nearly completed the task, the system provides assistance precisely when the user needs it least. Their hindsight optimization framework acts from the outset, distributing assistance across all plausible goals weighted by their posterior probability. Yet Demeester et al. (2008), whose greedy POMDP formulation implicitly delays aggressive intervention until intent becomes sufficiently clear, produces competitive outcomes — ten percent faster task completion than a reactive baseline — and does so within a user-adapted framework that respects individual driving profiles. The practical difference between these approaches in real wheelchair deployments, with matched populations and tasks, has not been resolved empirically. Ghorbel et al. (2017), whose system bridges both traditions, applies POMDP logic to the SmartWheeler platform and reports cognitive workload reduction, but does not provide a controlled comparison against a predict-then-act baseline. The methodological conditions needed to adjudicate this debate — a head-to-head comparison with the same participants, platform, and environment — do not exist in the current literature.

**Model-Based Versus Model-Free Intent Inference**

A second major disagreement concerns the appropriate representational substrate for intent inference. The Bayesian and POMDP tradition represented by Demeester et al. (2008), Javdani et al. (2018), and Ghorbel et al. (2017) relies on structured intent models: pre-specified goal sets, probabilistic user models, and interpretable belief representations. These models are transparent, auditable, and predictable — properties that matter for clinical certification and user trust — but require domain expertise to construct and break down when goal sets cannot be enumerated in advance. The deep reinforcement learning tradition represented by Reddy et al. (2018) and Chatzidimitriadis (2023) removes these requirements by learning end-to-end mappings from reward alone, substantially expanding applicability to open-ended real-world environments. The cost is a loss of interpretability: neither users nor clinicians can inspect why the system chose a particular intervention. Argall (2018) identifies transparency and explainability as among the critical clinical considerations that the autonomy community tends to overlook. No paper in the shortlist directly compares these algorithmic families in a wheelchair context with a clinical population, leaving the practical performance and acceptability tradeoff empirically unresolved.

**User Agency Versus Objective Performance Optimization**

A third tension concerns what shared autonomy systems should optimize for. The empirical literature consistently finds that higher autonomy improves objective outcomes: Erdogan and Argall (2017) show monotonically decreasing user effort as autonomy increases; Guo et al. (2025) quantify a 23.5 percent reaction time reduction at higher automation levels; Carlson and Demiris (2012) demonstrate collision elimination under collaborative control. The user-centered literature qualifies and complicates this picture. Viswanathan et al. (2017) find that cognitively impaired older adults explicitly prefer to remain in the control loop even in situations where autonomy would measurably improve outcomes, and articulate specific contexts in which autonomous assistance is unwelcome. Urdiales et al. (2011) operationalize a system that treats the user's contribution as independently valuable — blending rather than overriding — on the grounds that preserving agency is a design objective alongside performance. Aronson and Short (2024) add a further complication: users adapt their behavior in response to assistance, suggesting that the performance gains attributed to shared control may be partly explained by changed user strategy rather than system benefit. The tension between what users prefer and what maximizes objective performance is not resolved in this literature and is likely population-specific in ways that have not been systematically characterized.

**Unimpaired Proxies for Clinical Populations**

A fourth methodological debate concerns the validity of unimpaired participants as proxies for clinical wheelchair users. Recent papers — Xu et al. (2025) and Guo et al. (2025) — evaluate exclusively with unimpaired adults. Erdogan and Argall (2017) provide the clearest empirical evidence that this is problematic: SCI participants and uninjured controls diverged meaningfully in subjective preferences even when their objective performance was similar, demonstrating that findings from unimpaired samples do not transfer straightforwardly. How et al. (2013) identified a failure mode — audio prompt non-adherence — specific to cognitively impaired participants that would be invisible in neurotypical samples. Demeester et al. (2008) included Cerebral Palsy participants alongside healthy subjects but did not quantify the group-differential benefit. The field has not reached consensus on when unimpaired proxy studies are methodologically appropriate, what corrections or qualifications they require, or what effect sizes should be expected to transfer to clinical populations.

## 4. Open Questions and Research Gaps

- **Longitudinal adaptation and long-term benefit:** How does user performance, subjective preference, and control strategy evolve over weeks or months of real-world powered wheelchair use? No shortlisted paper tracks users beyond multiple sessions within a study protocol. Erdogan and Argall (2017) observe consistent improvement from session one to session two, and Boucher et al. (2013) span thirty-two sessions, but sustained community use over months remains entirely uncharted. This gap matters because both clinical decision-making (prescribing intelligent wheelchairs) and regulatory assessment require evidence of durable benefit.

- **Dynamic user modeling in response to assistance:** If users intentionally adapt their control strategies when assistance is active — as demonstrated by Aronson and Short (2024) — then intent models calibrated on unassisted behavior will be systematically miscalibrated the moment assistance is applied. No paper in the shortlist proposes or evaluates a framework in which the intent model itself continuously co-evolves with user strategy during normal use. Demeester et al. (2008) introduce user-adapted models but treat adaptation as a one-time personalization rather than an ongoing dynamic process. This gap matters because it calls into question the validity of nearly all evaluation studies in the field.

- **Head-to-head comparison of model-based and model-free approaches in wheelchair contexts:** Reddy et al. (2018) and Chatzidimitriadis (2023) demonstrate model-free deep RL shared autonomy; Demeester et al. (2008) and Javdani et al. (2018) demonstrate structured POMDP approaches. No paper directly compares these families with a clinical wheelchair population on matched tasks in a real environment. Argall (2018) identifies interpretability as a critical clinical consideration, and this gap leaves the performance-interpretability tradeoff empirically uncharacterized.

- **Clinical population diversity:** The shortlisted literature is disproportionately weighted toward two groups — individuals with SCI and older adults with cognitive impairment. People with ALS, multiple sclerosis, traumatic brain injury, progressive neuromuscular diseases, and pediatric motor impairments are largely absent. Argall (2018) explicitly identifies population heterogeneity as a defining design challenge, but the evidence base does not reflect this diversity. Systems optimized for SCI may be poorly suited to progressive conditions with fluctuating capability.

- **Transfer from simulation to regulated clinical deployment:** Guo et al. (2025) evaluate entirely in digital twin simulation; Chatzidimitriadis (2023) trains in simulation and tests in constrained real-world scenarios. Neither paper characterizes the magnitude of the simulation-to-reality gap or proposes a methodological pathway for bridging it under regulatory scrutiny. Argall (2018) notes that clinical considerations are often overlooked by the autonomy community; the specific evidentiary standards required for regulatory approval of shared autonomy as a medical device feature have not been addressed in any shortlisted paper.

- **User trust, technology acceptance, and automation complacency:** Viswanathan et al. (2017) document concerns about intelligent wheelchairs and the conditions under which users welcome or resist autonomous assistance. No paper in the shortlist rigorously measures trust development, over-reliance, automation complacency, or technology abandonment as primary outcomes over time. These factors are well-documented in the automation literature as determinants of real-world adoption and safety, and their absence from the wheelchair shared autonomy literature represents a significant gap for both research and clinical translation.

- **Standardized evaluation benchmarks and cross-study comparability:** Boucher et al. (2013) introduce the Robotic Wheelchair Skills Test as a standardized evaluation protocol, but subsequent papers do not consistently adopt it — making cross-study comparison of performance claims effectively impossible. Collision counts, task completion times, and cognitive load measures are reported across the literature, but with different task environments, population compositions, and operational definitions that prevent cumulative synthesis. The absence of a shared benchmark is identified implicitly across multiple papers but addressed directly by none.

- **Outdoor and community mobility environments:** Every shortlisted study was conducted indoors, in controlled corridors, simulated rooms, or digital twin environments. Community mobility — navigating sidewalks, crossings, elevators, public transit, and crowded social spaces — is precisely the context that determines quality-of-life impact for powered wheelchair users, and it is entirely unaddressed in the empirical literature. The collision-avoidance and intent-inference challenges in unstructured outdoor environments are qualitatively different from those in indoor navigation tasks.

- **Preservation versus attenuation of residual capability:** Whether shared autonomy preserves, attenuates, or rehabilitates residual motor and cognitive function over time is raised implicitly by the population-heterogeneity literature — Viswanathan et al. (2017) note that users want to remain in the control loop, and Argall (2018) gestures toward rehabilitation integration as a future goal — but it is never empirically tested across the shortlisted papers. For users with progressive conditions or incomplete injuries, this question has direct clinical significance.
